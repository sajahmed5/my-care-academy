## 1. Purpose

Covert medication means giving someone medicine in a disguised form — usually hidden in food or drink — so that they do not know they are taking it. It takes away a person's choice, so it is only ever a last resort. This policy sets out when {{organisation}} will give medicines covertly, the steps that must be taken first, and how we keep the decision under review.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who supports people with their medicines, and to the managers who arrange and review covert medication. It applies only to adults who lack the mental capacity to decide about their medicines.

It does not cover giving medicines in food or drink with the person's knowledge and agreement — for example, a person who prefers their tablets crushed in yoghurt because they find them hard to swallow. That is not covert, though the pharmacist should still advise whether a medicine can safely be given that way.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Mental Capacity Act 2005 and its Code of Practice — capacity is assessed for the specific decision, and anything done for a person who lacks capacity is in their best interests and the least restrictive option.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 11: Need for consent, and Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment, including the proper and safe management of medicines.
- NICE guideline NG67, Managing medicines for adults receiving social care in the community, and NICE guideline SC1, Managing medicines in care homes — covert administration only in exceptional circumstances, after a best interests decision involving the prescriber, and with a pharmacist's advice.
- CQC guidance on covert administration of medicines.
- Human Rights Act 1998, Article 8 — the right to respect for private life, which includes decisions about one's own body and treatment.

## 4. Our policy

- A person who has capacity to decide about their medicines and refuses them is never given them covertly. Their refusal is respected, recorded and reported to the prescriber.
- Covert medication is considered only when the person lacks capacity to decide about the medicine, refuses it, and needs it for their health — not for our convenience, and never to control behaviour without a proper clinical reason.
- It is never started by a care worker or care staff on their own. It needs a capacity assessment, a best interests decision involving the prescriber, a pharmacist's advice and a written plan.
- Each medicine is considered separately. A medicine that is not essential may be stopped instead.
- It is reviewed regularly, and stopped when it is no longer needed or no longer in the person's best interests.

## 5. Before covert medication is considered

When a person regularly refuses a medicine, the care worker or care staff record each refusal and tell the office or the shift leader. The registered manager makes sure the prescriber is told, and that other approaches are tried first:

- explaining again, at a different time, or by a different member of staff the person trusts;
- a different form of the medicine, such as a liquid, a patch or a dissolving tablet;
- changing the time of the dose to suit the person;
- asking the prescriber whether the medicine is still needed — some refusals end with a medicine being stopped.

## 6. How the decision is made

### 6.1 Capacity

A capacity assessment is carried out and recorded for the specific decision: whether to take this medicine. It is usually done by the prescriber, with the registered manager or a trained senior and the people who know the person best. If the person has capacity, covert medication is not an option. See Mental Capacity, Best Interests and Advocacy.

### 6.2 The best interests meeting

If the person lacks capacity, a best interests meeting is held. It includes the prescriber, the registered manager or a trained senior, and the person's family or representative. It also includes an attorney under a lasting power of attorney for health and welfare, or a deputy, who may make the decision themselves if it falls within their authority. Where the person has no one appropriate to speak for them, the registered manager asks whether an Independent Mental Capacity Advocate should be involved.

The meeting considers, for each medicine:

- why it is needed, and what would happen if it were not taken;
- what the person would want, as far as it can be known — their past wishes, beliefs and values;
- whether any less restrictive way has been tried;
- whether covert administration is in the person's best interests.

The decision, who took part and the reasons are recorded.

### 6.3 Pharmacist advice

Before any medicine is given covertly, a pharmacist advises how it can be given safely. Crushing tablets, opening capsules or mixing medicines with food can change how they work, make them harmful, or make them taste unpleasant enough to be refused. Some medicines — modified-release or coated tablets, for example — must never be crushed. Giving a medicine in a way not covered by its licence becomes the prescriber's responsibility, so the prescriber agrees to the method in writing.

### 6.4 The written plan

A covert medication plan is kept with the person's care plan and medication records. It sets out:

- each medicine to be given covertly, and the method for each — what it is mixed with, how much food or drink, and how it is prepared;
- that the medicine is offered openly first, and given covertly only if it is refused;
- what to do if the person does not finish the food or drink;
- who agreed the plan, with the date;
- the date it will be reviewed.

The plan is signed by the prescriber and the registered manager.

## 7. Giving the medicine

- Follow the plan exactly. Do not change the food, drink or method without the pharmacist's advice.
- Offer the medicine openly first, each time.
- Mix the medicine with a small amount of food or drink, so the person is likely to take all of it, and give it straight away.
- Record on the medication administration record that it was given covertly, how much the person took, and any concern.
- Never give covert medication that is not in the plan, and never add a new medicine to the plan yourself.

In home care, other people may live with or visit the person. The care worker explains, where appropriate, that food has medicine in it, so that no one else eats it. Family members who give medicines themselves are encouraged to follow the same plan.

## 8. Deprivation of liberty

Giving medicine covertly is a restriction on a person's freedom, and may form part of a deprivation of liberty. In a care home, the registered manager makes sure any DoLS application or authorisation mentions the covert medication, and follows any conditions attached to it. In a person's own home, the registered manager tells the local authority or NHS body that funds or arranges the care, which is responsible for seeking authorisation from the Court of Protection where needed. See Deprivation of Liberty.

## 9. Review

The plan is reviewed by the prescriber and the registered manager on the date it sets, at least every six months, and sooner when:

- the person's condition, capacity or wishes change;
- a medicine is added, stopped or changed;
- the person starts taking medicines willingly again;
- staff have any concern about how the plan is working.

Each review asks whether covert medication is still needed and still in the person's best interests, and is recorded.

## 10. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure covert medication is used only as this policy sets out, arranges meetings and reviews, and makes sure staff are trained.
- **Prescriber** — leads the capacity assessment for the medicine, takes part in the best interests decision, and authorises the method.
- **Pharmacist** — advises on whether and how each medicine can be given covertly.
- **Care workers and care staff** — record refusals, follow the plan exactly, and report concerns.

## 11. Training

Staff who give medicines complete medicines training and are assessed as competent before they give medicines alone, with competency recorded on their file. Before giving covert medication, they are shown the person's plan and how to follow it. Medicines training and competency are refreshed every year.

## 12. Records

The capacity assessment, best interests decision, pharmacist's advice, signed plan, reviews and medication administration records are kept on the person's care record for as long as we keep their record (see Record Keeping and Retention).
