## 1. Purpose

We work in people's own homes, at their invitation. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} asks for consent before giving care, respects clients who say no or change their mind, and acts lawfully when a client cannot consent for themselves.

## 2. Scope

It applies to every client, to everyone who works for us, and to everything we do with or for a client: entering their home, personal care, medicines, moving and handling, delegated healthcare tasks, photographs, monitoring and sharing information.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 11: Need for consent — care and treatment are given only with the person's consent or, where a person aged 16 or over lacks capacity, in line with the Mental Capacity Act 2005.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 and its Code of Practice — the five principles, assessing capacity, best interests decisions, lasting powers of attorney, deputies and advance decisions.
- Family Law Reform Act 1969, section 8 — a young person aged 16 or 17 can consent to their own medical treatment as an adult can.
- Human Rights Act 1998, Article 8 — the right to respect for private life and home.
- UK GDPR, the Data Protection Act 2018 and the common law duty of confidentiality — when we need consent to use or share information.
- CQC's Single Assessment Framework — the quality statement on consent to care and treatment, under the key question "effective".

## 4. Our policy

- We assume every client can make their own decisions unless it is shown, for a particular decision, that they cannot.
- We ask before every task, on every visit. Agreeing to a care plan is not agreeing to every task for ever.
- A client with capacity may refuse any task or a whole visit, or withdraw consent at any time, even if we think it unwise. We respect it.
- Nobody consents for a client who has capacity. Family members, including those who live with the client, cannot consent for them unless they hold a lasting power of attorney or deputyship covering that decision.

## 5. What valid consent is

Consent is valid when the client has capacity to make the decision, has the information they need in a way they understand, and agrees freely, without pressure from anyone. It can be given in different ways:

- Spoken — the client says yes when you explain what you would like to do.
- Written — the client signs or marks a record. We ask for it for how we get into the home and for photographs used for publicity. A signature does not make consent valid on its own; understanding and agreement do.
- Implied — the client shows agreement by what they do, such as opening the door to you or holding out their arm for cream. Silence, or simply going along with care, is not consent. If you are not sure, ask.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 Before care starts

At the assessment, the senior agrees the care plan with the client, recording their decisions, how they like to be asked, any lasting power of attorney, deputy or advance decision, who they want us to talk to, and anything they do not want shared. How care workers will get in is agreed and recorded (see Key Holding and Key Safes).

### 6.2 On every visit

- Knock or ring and announce yourself, even when you let yourself in. It is the client's home, and they decide which rooms you go into.
- Explain what you would like to do and why, and ask. Use the client's preferred language, simple words, gestures or pictures, as the care plan describes.
- Watch for signs that the client is no longer happy, and stop.

### 6.3 When a client says no or changes their mind

- Stop. Never force, trick or rush a client into care.
- Ask what is wrong. Offer another way, or to come back later if the rota allows.
- If a client refuses to let you in or asks you to leave, respect it, check as far as you can that they are safe, and phone the office or on-call before your next visit.
- If the refusal could cause harm — a missed medicine, no food or drink, a fall, pressure damage — explain the risk honestly and tell the office or on-call before you leave.
- Record what you offered, what the client said and what you did.
- If a client keeps refusing, the senior reviews the care plan with them and records an informed refusal as their choice (see Choice and Positive Risk-Taking). Refused medicines are reported to the prescriber.

### 6.4 When family disagree

Family help matters, but the decision belongs to the client. If a relative asks you to do something the client does not want, or to stop something they do want, do not argue in the home. Follow the care plan and the client's wishes, and tell the office. If you think someone is pressuring a client to agree or refuse, tell the office the same day: it may be a safeguarding concern.

### 6.5 When a client may not be able to consent

Capacity is about one decision at one time. If you think a client cannot understand, remember, weigh up or communicate a decision, tell the office. A trained senior or the registered manager assesses capacity for that decision and, if the client lacks it, a best interests decision is made with the people who know them, choosing the least restrictive option (see Mental Capacity, Best Interests and Advocacy). You must still explain what you are doing and involve the client as far as they can be.

### 6.6 Attorneys, deputies and advance decisions

An attorney under a lasting power of attorney for health and welfare may make the care decisions it covers, but only once the client lacks capacity to make them, and may refuse life-sustaining treatment only if the document says so. A lasting power of attorney for property and financial affairs gives no authority over care. A deputy appointed by the Court of Protection may make only the decisions the order sets out. The registered manager checks the document or the Office of the Public Guardian register and keeps a note on file.

A valid advance decision to refuse treatment that applies to the situation must be followed. Other wishes made in advance are not binding, but are weighed in every best interests decision. See End of Life Care, Death and Advance Decisions.

### 6.7 Photographs, monitoring and information

- Photographs for care, such as of skin damage for the district nurse, are taken only with consent or in the client's best interests, on a company device. Photographs for publicity need written consent (see Social Media, Phones and Technology).
- Door sensors, cameras or trackers that monitor a client, whoever arranged them, need the client's consent or a best interests decision. See Restraint and Restrictive Practice.
- We share information with the professionals involved in a client's care, and the client is told this when care starts. We share with family and friends only what the client agrees to, or what is in their best interests if they lack capacity (see Confidentiality).

### 6.8 Clients aged 16 or 17

A young person aged 16 or 17 is presumed to have capacity and can consent to their own care. Their parents may be involved with the young person's agreement. The young person cannot yet make a lasting power of attorney or advance decision. If they refuse care they need, or they and their parents disagree, the registered manager takes advice from the social worker responsible for them. See Safeguarding Children.

### 6.9 Deprivation of liberty

Where a client lacks capacity to consent to their care, and it may amount to a deprivation of liberty, it must be authorised by the Court of Protection. The registered manager tells the local authority or NHS body that funds or arranges the care in writing, so that it can apply. Since the Supreme Court's judgment in A Reference by the Attorney General for Northern Ireland (June 2026), which overruled the 2014 Cheshire West judgment, and the Department of Health and Social Care's guidance on it, this depends on the client's whole situation, including whether they object. See Deprivation of Liberty.

## 7. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure consent is sought and recorded, capacity is assessed where in doubt, attorneys' and deputies' authority is checked, and possible deprivations of liberty are referred to the commissioner.
- **Seniors** — agree care plans and access arrangements with clients, and review them when a client keeps refusing.
- **Office and on-call staff** — act the same day on harmful refusals and concerns about capacity or pressure.
- **Care workers** — ask before every task, respect refusals, record them, and report concerns before leaving the visit.

## 8. Training

All staff learn about consent and the Mental Capacity Act during induction, as part of the Care Certificate, and refresh it every year. Staff who assess capacity complete further training first. Training is recorded on the training matrix.

## 9. Records

Care plan agreements, access arrangements, refusals, capacity assessments, best interests decisions, notes of attorneys, deputies and advance decisions, and consent for photographs and sharing information are kept on the client's record for as long as our retention schedule requires (see Record Keeping and Retention).
