## 1. Purpose

Many people living in a care home cannot decide for themselves whether to live there, and are cared for in ways that keep them safe but limit their freedom: a keypad on the front door, staff who would stop them leaving, constant supervision. In law this may be a deprivation of liberty, and it must be authorised. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} uses the Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards so that nobody is deprived of their liberty here without lawful authority.

## 2. Scope

It applies to every resident aged 18 or over, and to everyone who works in the home. For a person aged 16 or 17, the Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards do not apply, and an order of the Court of Protection is needed instead; the registered manager asks the local authority to apply.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Human Rights Act 1998 and Article 5 of the European Convention on Human Rights — the right to liberty.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005, Schedule A1 — the Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards (DoLS): the care home, as the managing authority, asks the local authority, as the supervisory body, to authorise a deprivation of liberty.
- The Supreme Court's judgment in P v Cheshire West and Chester Council (2014) — the "acid test" for when care amounts to a deprivation of liberty.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 13: Safeguarding service users from abuse and improper treatment — a person must not be deprived of their liberty for the purpose of care without lawful authority.
- Care Quality Commission (Registration) Regulations 2009, Regulation 18 — we notify CQC without delay of the outcome of each request for a DoLS authorisation.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 Code of Practice and Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards Code of Practice.

The Liberty Protection Safeguards, which were meant to replace DoLS, have not been brought into force. DoLS remains the law in care homes.

## 4. What a deprivation of liberty is

A resident is deprived of their liberty when they lack the capacity to consent to living here and being cared for here, they are under continuous supervision and control, and they are not free to leave — meaning to move away permanently. It does not matter that they seem content, are not trying to leave, or that the arrangements are kind and in their best interests.

In practice, a resident who lacks capacity to decide where to live, cannot go out without staff, and would be brought back if they tried to leave, is likely to be deprived of their liberty. Other signs include sensor mats or door alarms used to monitor them, bed rails or lap belts, medicine to calm them, and restrictions on who they may see.

## 5. Our policy

- We look for the least restrictive way to keep each resident safe, and review restrictions regularly.
- Where a resident's care amounts to a deprivation of liberty, we apply for an authorisation before it starts, or as soon as we realise it has started.
- We never use the existence of an authorisation as a reason to restrict a resident more than they need.
- We comply with every condition attached to an authorisation.
- Residents and their representatives are told their rights and helped to use them.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 Before admission and on review

At the pre-admission assessment, and at every care plan review, the registered manager or senior considers whether the resident's care will amount to a deprivation of liberty. A capacity assessment about the decision to live here and receive this care is completed and recorded where capacity is in doubt.

### 6.2 Requesting a standard authorisation

If the resident's care will amount to a deprivation of liberty, {{registered_manager}} requests a standard authorisation from the supervisory body — normally the local authority where the resident is ordinarily resident — using that authority's forms. Where possible the request is made before the resident moves in.

The supervisory body arranges the assessments the law requires, including of the resident's age, mental health, capacity and best interests, and whether there is any valid refusal by an attorney, deputy or advance decision. Staff help the assessors: they give them access to the resident and the records, and tell them what they know about the resident's wishes.

### 6.3 Urgent authorisations

If a resident needs to be deprived of their liberty before a standard authorisation can be obtained — for example, after an emergency admission — the registered manager, or the senior in charge if they cannot be reached, may grant an urgent authorisation. It lasts for up to seven days. A request for a standard authorisation must be made at the same time. If the supervisory body has not decided before the urgent authorisation ends, the registered manager may ask it to extend the urgent authorisation, once, for up to seven more days, giving the reasons.

An urgent authorisation is for genuine urgency. It is recorded on the form, and a copy is kept on the resident's record and given to the resident and their representative.

### 6.4 When an authorisation is granted

The registered manager:

- reads the authorisation and records its start and end dates and any conditions in the care plan;
- makes sure staff on every shift know what the conditions are and follow them;
- gives the resident information about the authorisation and their rights, in a way they can understand, and makes sure their relevant person's representative — the person appointed to represent and support them — has it too;
- notifies CQC of the outcome without delay;
- diarises the end date and requests a further authorisation in good time before it expires, if one is still needed.

### 6.5 When an authorisation is refused

If the supervisory body refuses, the resident must not be deprived of their liberty. The registered manager notifies CQC of the outcome and, with the supervisory body, the resident's family and professionals, changes the care so that it is no longer a deprivation of liberty, or agrees another lawful way forward. If the resident cannot safely stay here without one, the local authority must be involved at once.

### 6.6 Changes and reviews

Ask the supervisory body to review an authorisation when the resident's needs or restrictions change, when a condition is no longer workable, or when the resident regains capacity. If the resident's care stops amounting to a deprivation of liberty, tell the supervisory body.

### 6.7 Rights and challenges

Residents subject to an authorisation have the right to challenge it in the Court of Protection. Their relevant person's representative helps them, and an Independent Mental Capacity Advocate may support them or their representative. Staff must tell the registered manager if a resident says they want to leave or seems to object, so that the representative is told. We do not discourage anyone from challenging an authorisation.

### 6.8 Care staff

Know which residents have an authorisation and what its conditions say. Report any restriction that is not in the care plan. Never use more restriction than the care plan allows. If you think a resident is deprived of their liberty without an authorisation, tell the shift leader the same day.

## 7. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — identifies residents who need an authorisation, makes requests, grants urgent authorisations, notifies CQC, makes sure conditions are met, and tracks expiry dates.
- **Shift leaders** — grant an urgent authorisation if the registered manager cannot be reached, make sure conditions are followed on their shift, and pass on concerns.
- **Care staff** — know the restrictions and conditions for each resident, keep restrictions to the minimum, and report concerns.

## 8. Training

All staff learn about the Mental Capacity Act and the Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards in induction and refresh it every year. The registered manager and shift leaders who may grant urgent authorisations complete further training on DoLS first.

## 9. Records

Capacity assessments, requests, urgent authorisations, standard authorisations and their conditions, refusals, correspondence with the supervisory body, and CQC notifications are kept on the resident's record. The registered manager keeps a log of every resident with an application or authorisation, its status and its end date. Records are kept for as long as our retention schedule requires (see Record Keeping and Retention).
