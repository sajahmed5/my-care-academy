## 1. Purpose

A care plan is the instruction every care worker follows when they walk into a client's home, often on their own and often for the first time. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} writes care plans with clients, so that the plan describes the person and the life they want, tells care workers exactly what to do on each visit, and stays up to date as things change.

## 2. Scope

It applies to every client we support, to everyone who writes or reviews care plans, and to every care worker who follows them.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 9: Person-centred care — care is appropriate, meets the person's needs and reflects their preferences, and they are involved in planning it.
- Regulation 11: Need for consent — the plan is agreed with the person, or made in line with the Mental Capacity Act.
- Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment — risks are assessed and the plan says how they are reduced.
- Regulation 17: Good governance — records about each person are accurate, complete and up to date.
- Care Act 2014 — the wellbeing principle, and the local authority's care and support plan, which ours must fit with.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — people are supported to make their own decisions about their care.
- Accessible Information Standard — the plan records how the person communicates and the support they need.
- NICE guideline NG21, Home care: delivering personal care and practical support to older people living in their own homes.

## 4. Our policy

- The plan is written with the client, in their words where possible. It starts with who they are and what they want, not with tasks.
- It says what the client can do for themselves, so that care workers support independence rather than take it away.
- Every visit has clear instructions: what to do, in what order, and how the client likes it done.
- Every risk in the plan has an agreed way of reducing it, and the client's right to take reasonable risks is respected.
- The client, and anyone they choose, can see their plan whenever they want.
- The plan is reviewed when things change, and at least every year.

## 5. What a care plan contains

### 5.1 About the person

- How they like to be addressed, and the people who matter to them.
- Their life history, interests, routines and what makes a good day for them, as much as they want to share.
- What they want our support to achieve — staying at home, getting out again, managing after a stroke.
- How they communicate, any sensory loss, and their preferred language.
- Their culture, faith and any preferences about who gives personal care.

### 5.2 Their decisions

- Whether they have agreed to the plan, or how decisions are made if they cannot.
- Any lasting power of attorney, deputy or advance decision, and what it covers.
- Any decisions they have made that we have discussed with them because they carry a risk.

### 5.3 Each visit

For each visit: the time and length, the number of care workers, and the tasks in order, written so a new care worker could follow them — for example, "Ask Mrs Shah if she would like a shower or a wash. She prefers a shower on Monday and Thursday. She washes her face and front herself; help with her back, legs and feet." Avoid "assist with personal care".

### 5.4 Health and safety

- Medicines support, at the level agreed, with a current list.
- Moving and handling, with the equipment and method for each transfer.
- Eating and drinking, skin care, continence and falls, where they apply.
- The home environment and access, and risks to the care worker.
- Who to contact — family, GP, district nurse, pharmacy — and what the client wants us to tell them.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 Writing the plan

A trained senior writes the first plan from the assessment, with the client and anyone they want involved. Before care starts, the parts that keep the client and care workers safe are in place; the rest is added over the first weeks as we get to know them. The senior reads the plan back to the client, or shares it in a format they can use, and records whether they agree.

### 6.2 Where the plan is kept

The plan is kept on the client's record and is available to every care worker before each visit. A copy is kept in the client's home where they agree, in a place they choose. Treat it as confidential: do not leave it where visitors can read it.

### 6.3 Before and during each visit

- Read the plan before your first visit to a client, and check the notes for changes before every visit.
- Follow the plan, asking the client at each step. The plan describes what they usually want; it does not replace asking.
- If the client wants something done differently today, and it is safe, do it their way and record it.
- If something in the plan cannot be done, or does not seem right, do not guess. Phone the office or on-call.

### 6.4 Daily records

At the end of each visit, record what you did, what the client ate and drank if the plan asks, how they seemed, and anything that has changed or worried you. Write facts, in plain language, as if the client were reading it — they may. Record medicines on the medicines record, not only in the notes.

### 6.5 Telling us about changes

Phone the office the same day if a client's needs change: they need more help to stand, they are more confused, they are not eating, a family carer is no longer helping, or the visit times no longer suit them. The office passes it to the senior, who updates the plan and tells the care workers on that run.

### 6.6 Reviews

A senior reviews the plan with the client:

- after the first few weeks of care;
- after a fall, a hospital stay, a new diagnosis, or any significant change;
- when the client, their family or a care worker asks;
- and in any case at least every year.

At each review we ask the client what is working and what is not, update the plan, and record the review even if nothing changes. If the client needs more or less time than is commissioned, we tell the local authority or the integrated care board and ask them to review their plan. Care workers are told about every change before their next visit.

### 6.7 When the client cannot agree

If the client cannot agree to part of their plan, that part is made in their best interests, following the Mental Capacity Act, with the people who know them. The plan says which decisions were made this way and why. See Mental Capacity, Best Interests and Advocacy.

## 7. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure every client has a current plan, checks a sample of plans and daily records for quality, and makes sure reviews happen on time.
- **Senior or care coordinator** — writes and reviews plans with clients, tells care workers about changes, and acts on what care workers report.
- **Office staff** — pass on changes the same day and keep the rota in line with the plan.
- **Care workers** — read and follow the plan, ask the client, keep good daily records and report changes.

## 8. Training

All care workers learn how to read and follow a care plan and keep daily records during induction, as part of the Care Certificate. Staff who write and review plans are trained in person-centred planning and risk assessment, and their first plans are checked by the registered manager. Spot checks look at whether care workers follow the plan, and are recorded on the person's file.

## 9. Records

Care plans, risk assessments, reviews and daily records are kept on the client's record. When a plan changes, the earlier version is kept so we can show what was in place at any time. Records are kept for as long as our retention schedule requires (see Record Keeping and Retention).
