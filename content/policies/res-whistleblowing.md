## 1. Purpose

Whistleblowing means raising a concern about wrongdoing, danger or malpractice at work that affects other people: the residents, colleagues or the public. In a care home, poor practice can become normal for a team, and many residents cannot complain for themselves. Staff who speak up are often the only way it comes to light. This policy tells everyone who works for {{organisation}} how to raise a concern, what we will do about it, and how the law and this policy protect them.

## 2. Scope

It applies to all employees and workers in the home, on every shift, including bank and agency workers. The law protects employees and workers, including agency workers. Volunteers, students on placement and contractors may not be covered by the law, but this policy protects them in exactly the same way.

A complaint about your own pay, contract or treatment is a grievance (see Grievance), unless it also affects others. A concern that a resident is being abused or neglected is reported under our Safeguarding policy. Both are protected: you will never be treated less well for using either.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Employment Rights Act 1996, Part IVA, inserted by the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 — the protection for workers who make a protected disclosure, and the right not to be dismissed or suffer any detriment for it.
- Enterprise and Regulatory Reform Act 2013 — a disclosure qualifies only if the worker reasonably believes it is made in the public interest. Whether it was made in good faith no longer decides whether it is protected; an employment tribunal may only reduce compensation if it was not.
- Employment Rights Act 2025 — since 6 April 2026, a disclosure that sexual harassment has occurred, is occurring or is likely to occur can be a qualifying disclosure.
- Public Interest Disclosure (Prescribed Persons) Order 2014 — the regulators and other bodies to which a worker can make a protected disclosure, and the matters each covers.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 17: Good governance — we seek and act on feedback, including from staff, to improve the service.
- CQC's Single Assessment Framework — the well-led quality statement on freedom to speak up: staff feel able to speak up and are listened to.

## 4. What the law protects

A disclosure qualifies when you pass on information that you reasonably believe shows that one or more of these has happened, is happening or is likely to happen:

- a criminal offence;
- a failure to meet a legal obligation;
- a miscarriage of justice;
- a danger to anyone's health or safety;
- damage to the environment;
- sexual harassment;
- the deliberate hiding of information about any of these.

You must also reasonably believe that raising it is in the public interest: that it matters to other people, not only to you. You do not need proof, and you do not need to be right. You need a reasonable belief.

In a care home, concerns might include call bells ignored or switched off; residents got up or put to bed to suit the rota; too few staff on a shift, especially at night; residents restrained, locked in or given medicines covertly without proper authority; records of repositioning or fluids filled in for care not given; fire doors wedged open; residents' money going missing; a colleague asleep on a night shift; or sexual harassment of staff.

## 5. Our policy

- We want to hear concerns early, while they can be put right. Speaking up is part of your duty of care.
- Every concern is taken seriously, looked into and answered.
- We keep your identity confidential if you ask us to.
- No one is dismissed, disciplined, given fewer shifts or worse shifts, or treated less well in any way because they raised a concern. Victimising someone who has spoken up is a disciplinary matter.
- No contract, settlement agreement or confidentiality clause of ours stops anyone making a protected disclosure. The law makes any clause that tries to do so void.
- A concern that turns out to be mistaken is never held against you. Only an allegation you know to be false, made to cause harm, falls outside this policy and may lead to disciplinary action.
- We guard against a closed culture. Managers spend time on every shift, including nights and weekends, and may visit unannounced to see for themselves.

## 6. How to raise a concern

### 6.1 Inside the company

Raise it as soon as you can. If a resident is at risk now, tell the shift leader straight away and make them safe; do not wait to raise a whistleblowing concern.

- You can tell the shift leader, or go straight to {{registered_manager}}, on {{office_phone}} or at {{office_email}}, in person, by phone or in writing. You do not have to go through the shift leader, and you should not if the concern is about them.
- On a night or weekend shift, phone the on-call manager.
- If the concern is about the registered manager, or you have raised it and nothing has happened, go to {{nominated_individual}}.
- If it is about the nominated individual or the owners, go to one of the bodies in section 6.3.

Say what happened, when, where, who was involved and who else saw it. Do not investigate yourself, look in records you do not normally use, or take copies of residents' records; tell us where the evidence is. You may bring a colleague or trade union representative to any meeting about your concern.

### 6.2 Anonymously

You can raise a concern without giving your name. We will look into it as far as we can, but it is harder to investigate and we cannot tell you the outcome. Giving your name and asking us to keep it confidential usually works better. In a small team, we take care that how we look into a concern does not point to who raised it.

### 6.3 Outside the company

You can go to a prescribed person, a body named in law to receive disclosures, without raising it with us first, if you reasonably believe the matter is one it deals with and that what you say is substantially true. They include:

- the Care Quality Commission, for the care in the home and how it is run, on 03000 616161;
- the Health and Safety Executive, for risks to health and safety at work;
- the Information Commission, for the misuse or loss of personal information;
- your Member of Parliament, for any matter.

Other bodies are prescribed for other matters; Protect can tell you which one deals with your concern. A concern that a resident is being abused or neglected can also be reported straight to the local authority's adult safeguarding team, or to the police where a crime is involved.

Going to the media or posting on social media is protected only in narrow circumstances, and can breach residents' confidentiality. Take advice first.

### 6.4 Advice

Protect, the whistleblowing charity, gives free, confidential advice on 020 3117 2520. Acas, or your trade union, can also advise. Asking a lawyer for advice is itself a protected disclosure.

## 7. What happens next

- The person who receives your concern acknowledges it within five working days, in writing if you wish.
- They decide how it will be looked into, and by whom: normally someone not involved and not working on the same shifts. A risk to a resident is acted on, and referred under our Safeguarding policy, the same day.
- If it involves someone's conduct, it may also lead to action under our Disciplinary policy.
- We tell you what we have done and whether the concern was upheld, as far as we can without breaching anyone else's confidentiality. We aim to do so within 20 working days, or tell you why it is taking longer.
- If you are not satisfied, raise it with {{nominated_individual}}, or go to one of the bodies in section 6.3.

## 8. Confidentiality and protection

We will not reveal your identity without your agreement unless the law requires it, for example in a police investigation or a court case. If that happens, we will tell you first wherever we can.

If you believe you are being treated less well because you raised a concern, by a manager, a shift leader, a colleague or anyone else, tell {{nominated_individual}} at once. Being frozen out by the team is detriment too. The law gives you the right not to suffer any detriment for making a protected disclosure, including from colleagues, and a dismissal because of one is automatically unfair, however long you have worked for us. You can bring a claim to an employment tribunal; the time limits are short, so take advice early.

## 9. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — receives and looks into concerns, keeps them confidential, makes sure nobody suffers for raising one, and reports themes to the nominated individual.
- **Nominated individual** — receives concerns about the registered manager and complaints of victimisation, and checks that concerns are acted on.
- **Shift leaders and on-call managers** — make residents safe, pass concerns straight to the registered manager, and never discourage anyone from speaking up.
- **All staff** — speak up, support colleagues who do, and never victimise anyone for it.

## 10. Training

All staff learn this policy at induction, alongside safeguarding, and it is revisited at supervision and team meetings. Shift leaders and managers are trained in receiving concerns and protecting the people who raise them.

## 11. Records

The registered manager records each concern, how it was looked into, the outcome and what the person who raised it was told. Records are kept securely, apart from the staff files of the people involved, seen only by those who need to, and kept for the periods set in Record Keeping and Retention. The number and themes of concerns, without names, are reviewed in our governance checks.
