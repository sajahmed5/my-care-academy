## 1. Purpose

Everyone who works for {{organisation}} has the right to be treated with dignity and respect. This policy sets out what bullying and harassment are, what we do to prevent them, including the steps we take to prevent sexual harassment, and how to raise a concern and have it dealt with.

## 2. Scope

It applies to all staff, and to agency workers, students and volunteers who work with us. It covers behaviour at work, on visits and in the care home, at training and work social events, and online, including in messaging groups and on social media, wherever it affects someone at work. It covers harassment by colleagues and managers, and by the people we support, their families and visitors.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Equality Act 2010 — protection from harassment related to a protected characteristic, from sexual harassment, and from victimisation for complaining. Since October 2024, employers have a duty to take reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment of their employees in the course of their employment, including by third parties such as clients and their families.
- Equality and Human Rights Commission, technical guidance on sexual harassment and harassment at work — what reasonable steps look like.
- Protection from Harassment Act 1997 — a course of conduct that causes alarm or distress is a criminal offence and a civil wrong.
- Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 — our duty to protect employees' health, including from stress caused by bullying.
- Acas guidance on bullying and harassment at work.

## 4. What we mean

Bullying is offensive, intimidating, malicious or insulting behaviour, or misuse of power, that undermines, humiliates or harms someone. It is often repeated, but a single serious act can be bullying. Examples include shouting, belittling someone in front of others, spreading rumours, leaving someone out on purpose, setting impossible rotas or targets as a punishment, and unfair criticism.

Harassment is unwanted conduct related to a protected characteristic — age, disability, gender reassignment, race, religion or belief, sex or sexual orientation — that violates someone's dignity or creates an intimidating, hostile, degrading, humiliating or offensive environment for them. It does not matter whether it was meant that way: what matters is its effect, and whether it was reasonable for it to have that effect.

Sexual harassment is unwanted conduct of a sexual nature with that effect — for example comments about someone's body, sexual jokes, unwanted touching, or sharing sexual images. Treating someone less favourably because they rejected or gave in to it is also harassment.

Reasonable management — fair feedback, setting standards, or dealing with poor performance through the proper process — is not bullying.

## 5. Our policy

- Bullying and harassment are not tolerated, and are dealt with as misconduct. Serious cases may be gross misconduct.
- Everyone who raises a concern in good faith, or helps with an investigation, is protected from victimisation.
- We take reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment, and do not wait for a complaint before acting.

## 6. How we prevent it

- This policy is set for everyone to read and accept, and is part of induction.
- Managers are trained to recognise and deal with bullying and harassment.
- We assess the risk of sexual harassment, including on visits and night shifts, and act on what the assessment shows — for example, changing who visits a client, or making sure no one works alone with someone who has behaved inappropriately.
- We tell the people we support and their families, through our service information, that we expect our staff to be treated with respect.
- We ask about how people are treated at supervision and appraisal, and look at what concerns and exit interviews tell us.

## 7. What to do

### 7.1 If it is happening to you

If you feel able to, tell the person that their behaviour is unwelcome and ask them to stop. You do not have to do this. Keep a note of what happened, when, and who saw it.

Tell your manager, or {{registered_manager}} if the concern is about your manager. You can raise it informally, or formally under our Grievance policy, from your own login or in writing. A complaint about a manager is handled by someone else, and that manager does not see it.

If you have been assaulted or threatened, or fear for your safety, phone 999.

### 7.2 Informal resolution

Where you want it, and it is appropriate, the manager may speak to the person, or arrange a facilitated conversation, to stop the behaviour quickly. You do not have to take this route, and serious cases are dealt with formally.

### 7.3 Formal complaints

A formal complaint is investigated promptly and fairly under our Grievance policy. You may be accompanied at meetings. While it is investigated, we may change rotas or duties to keep the people involved apart — wherever possible without disadvantaging the person who complained. Where the complaint is upheld, action is taken against the person responsible under our Disciplinary policy.

### 7.4 Harassment by people we support or their families

Some behaviour comes from illness — for example dementia, a brain injury or delirium. It still matters, and you should still report it. Tell the office or the person in charge the same day.

The manager looks at what happened, records it through the site's concerns process, and decides with you what to do. This may include speaking to the person or their family, reviewing the care plan and risk assessment with professionals involved, sending two staff or a different worker, or, as a last resort in homecare, ending the service. Where someone with capacity harasses staff, we tell them clearly that it must stop. Where a crime may have been committed, we support you to report it to the police.

You will not be blamed for reporting behaviour, and you will not be expected to keep supporting someone who has harassed you if you do not feel safe to.

## 8. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure this policy is followed, that the risk of sexual harassment is assessed and acted on, and that complaints are dealt with promptly and fairly.
- **Managers** — set an example, act on what they see without waiting for a complaint, and support staff who raise concerns.
- **All staff** — treat others with respect, challenge or report bullying and harassment, and co-operate with investigations.

## 9. Training

All staff learn about this policy at induction. Managers are trained in handling complaints of bullying and harassment, and in their duty to prevent sexual harassment, and refresh it as the training matrix sets.

## 10. Records

Complaints, investigations and outcomes are kept confidentially in the HR pack. Incidents involving people we support are recorded through the concerns process. Our risk assessment for sexual harassment, and what we did about it, is kept and reviewed every year. Records are kept for six years after the employment of those involved ends.
