## 1. Purpose

The water in a care home can harm residents in two ways. Legionella bacteria can grow in water systems and cause Legionnaires' disease, a serious pneumonia to which older and frail people are especially vulnerable. And water that is hot enough to control legionella is hot enough to scald someone who cannot feel it, react to it or get out of the bath. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} manages both risks.

## 2. Scope

It applies to the hot and cold water systems in the home, and to everyone who manages, maintains or uses them — the registered manager, maintenance staff, contractors, and care staff who help residents to wash and bathe.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 — our duty to protect residents, staff and visitors from risks arising from our premises and work.
- Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations 2002 — legionella is a biological agent, and the risk from it must be assessed and controlled.
- Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999 — the duty to assess risks and appoint competent help.
- Health and Safety Executive Approved Code of Practice L8, Legionnaires' disease: the control of legionella bacteria in water systems — the legal standard for assessing and controlling the risk.
- HSE technical guidance HSG274 Part 2, The control of legionella bacteria in hot and cold water systems — how to do it in practice, including temperatures and checks.
- HSE guidance on managing the risks from hot water and surfaces in health and social care.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment, and Regulation 15: Premises and equipment.

## 4. Our policy

- A competent person carries out a written legionella risk assessment for the home, and we follow the written scheme of control it produces.
- A named responsible person manages water safety day to day.
- Hot water is kept hot enough to control legionella in the system, and blended to a safe temperature at every outlet residents use.
- Every bath and shower is checked with a thermometer before a resident gets in.
- Checks are done when they are due, and recorded.

## 5. Who is responsible

{{organisation}}, as the employer and the body in control of the premises, is the duty holder. {{registered_manager}} appoints a responsible person to manage the legionella risk day to day, who has the training and time to do so, and who can call on a competent water hygiene contractor for specialist work.

> Name your responsible person for water safety and their deputy, and your water hygiene contractor if you use one.

## 6. The risk assessment and scheme of control

The legionella risk assessment describes the water system, identifies where bacteria could grow — where water is stored or stagnant, is between 20°C and 45°C, or there is scale, rust or sludge — and identifies the residents most at risk. It sets out a written scheme of control: what must be checked, how often, by whom, and what to do when a result is outside the expected range.

The risk assessment is reviewed regularly, and straight away when:

- the water system is altered, or a part of the building is taken out of use or brought back into use;
- the people at risk change — for example, residents with weaker immune systems;
- monitoring shows the controls are not working;
- there is a case of Legionnaires' disease linked to the home.

## 7. Controlling legionella

The scheme of control follows HSG274. The main controls are:

- **Temperature** — hot water is stored at 60°C or above and circulated so that it reaches 50°C or above within a minute of running at outlets (before any mixing valve). Cold water is kept below 20°C. Temperatures are checked and recorded at the intervals the scheme sets, including at the outlets nearest and furthest from the tanks.
- **Flushing** — taps and showers that are used less than once a week, including in empty rooms, are run every week, and the flushing recorded. Little-used outlets are removed where possible.
- **Cleaning** — shower heads and hoses are cleaned and descaled at the intervals the scheme sets, usually every three months.
- **Tanks and calorifiers** — cold water storage tanks are inspected and cleaned, and hot water cylinders drained and checked, as the scheme sets out.
- **Sampling** — water is tested for legionella where the risk assessment says it is needed, or when monitoring gives cause for concern.

When a room is empty, the maintenance staff flush its outlets weekly until a new resident moves in, and flush them again on the day the room is reoccupied.

## 8. Preventing scalds

### 8.1 Thermostatic mixing valves

Every bath, shower and wash-hand basin residents use is fitted with a thermostatic mixing valve, which blends hot and cold water to a safe temperature and shuts off the hot water if the cold supply fails. HSE advises that hot water at outlets used by vulnerable people should not exceed 44°C. Valves are set by a competent person, checked regularly, and serviced as the manufacturer recommends, including a test that they shut off safely.

### 8.2 Before every bath and shower

- Run the water and check the temperature with the thermometer provided, every time, before the resident gets in. Do not rely on your hand.
- If the water is too hot, or the temperature varies, do not use it. Tell the maintenance staff or the shift leader, and use another bath or shower.
- Never leave a resident alone in the bath or shower unless their risk assessment says it is safe.
- Do not top up the bath with hot water while the resident is in it.
- Record the temperature as the home's procedure requires.

### 8.3 Hot surfaces

Radiators and exposed hot pipes that a resident could touch, fall against or become trapped against are covered, or are low-temperature types, as the premises risk assessment says.

## 9. If something goes wrong

- If a check finds water outside the expected temperature, the responsible person investigates the same day and follows the scheme of control.
- If a resident is scalded, cool the burn under cool running water, call 111 or 999 depending on how serious it is, and tell the shift leader. Take the bath or shower out of use until it has been checked. Record it through the site's concerns process; a scald is likely to need a CQC notification and a safeguarding referral (see Reportable Incidents).
- If a resident or member of staff is diagnosed with Legionnaires' disease, the registered manager contacts the UK Health Security Agency health protection team straight away and follows its advice, tells the water hygiene contractor, and considers whether a report to the Health and Safety Executive is needed.

## 10. Roles and responsibilities

- **Nominated individual** — makes sure the duty holder's responsibilities are met and the resources are available.
- **Registered manager** — appoints the responsible person, makes sure the risk assessment is done and reviewed, and checks that the scheme of control is followed.
- **Responsible person and maintenance staff** — carry out and record the checks, flush little-used outlets, maintain mixing valves, and act on results.
- **Care staff** — check the temperature before every bath and shower, report faults, and never use an outlet that is too hot.

## 11. Training

The responsible person and maintenance staff complete legionella awareness and control training before taking on the role, and refresh it as their training plan sets out. All care staff are trained in safe bathing and scald prevention at induction, and refresh it every year with their health and safety training. Training is recorded on the training matrix.

## 12. Records

The risk assessment, the scheme of control, the names of the responsible person and contractors, and the results of every check, service and sample are kept in the water safety log for at least five years, as the Approved Code of Practice requires. Bath and shower temperature checks are kept as the home's procedure sets out.
