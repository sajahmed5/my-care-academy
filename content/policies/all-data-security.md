## 1. Purpose

Care records hold some of the most sensitive information there is. A lost phone, a weak password or a convincing fake email can expose it, or stop us getting to it when someone needs care. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} keeps information and systems secure, and what we do when something goes wrong.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works for us, and to all systems, devices and information we use: the site, care planning and rota systems, email, phones, tablets and computers — company-owned or personal devices used for work — and paper records.

## 3. The law and guidance

- UK GDPR, Article 32 — appropriate security for personal information; Articles 33 and 34 — reporting personal data breaches to the Information Commissioner's Office and telling the people affected.
- Data Protection Act 2018.
- Computer Misuse Act 1990 — accessing a computer system or information without authority is a criminal offence.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 17: Good governance — records kept securely.
- Data Security and Protection Toolkit — the yearly self-assessment against the National Data Guardian's data security standards, which care providers are expected to complete.
- National Cyber Security Centre guidance on passwords, devices and phishing.

## 4. Our policy

- We complete the Data Security and Protection Toolkit every year and act on what it shows.
- Every person has their own login. Logins are never shared.
- Access is given only to what each person needs, and removed promptly when they change role or leave.
- Devices used for work are protected, kept up to date and can be locked or wiped if lost.
- Every breach, or suspected breach, is reported at once, however small, and nobody is blamed for reporting one.

## 5. What to do

### 5.1 Passwords and logins

- Use a strong password for each system — three random words joined together is a good way to make one.
- Never use your work password for anything else.
- Never tell anyone your password, write it where others can find it, or let anyone use your login. The office will never ask for it.
- Turn on multi-factor authentication — a code or prompt on your phone as well as your password — wherever a system offers it. It is required for email and for any system holding care records, where available.
- Lock your screen whenever you step away, and log out when you have finished.

### 5.2 Devices

- Company phones and tablets have a passcode, encryption and up-to-date software, and are set up so they can be found, locked or wiped remotely.
- A personal phone may be used for work only in the ways the company allows: with a screen lock, up-to-date software, and no care information saved to the phone itself, its photos or its downloads.
- Do not install apps on a company device unless they are approved.
- Do not use public Wi-Fi for care records without the company's approved protection; use mobile data instead.
- Do not plug in unknown USB sticks or devices.
- Report a lost or stolen device to the office straight away, day or night.

### 5.3 Email and phishing

Phishing is a fake email, text or call designed to trick you into clicking a link, opening an attachment, giving a password or making a payment. Warning signs include urgency, an unexpected request, a sender's address that does not quite match, and a request to log in through a link.

- Do not click links or open attachments you were not expecting. If in doubt, contact the sender through a number or address you already know.
- Never give your password in reply to an email, text or call.
- Report anything suspicious to the office, and do not forward it to colleagues.
- Check the address before you send an email with personal information, and use secure email where it is available for sending care information to other organisations.
- Requests to change bank details or make an urgent payment are always checked by phone with a known contact.

### 5.4 Paper records

Keep paper records locked away when not in use, carry only what you need, and never leave them in a car. Dispose of them through confidential waste, never in an ordinary bin.

## 6. Breaches

### 6.1 What a breach is

A personal data breach is a breach of security that leads to personal information being lost, destroyed, changed, disclosed or accessed without authority, by accident or on purpose. Examples are a lost phone, an email or message sent to the wrong person, a care plan left in a public place, a colleague looking at a record they do not need, a cyber attack, or being locked out of records by ransomware.

### 6.2 Reporting it

Tell {{registered_manager}}, or on-call out of hours, straight away. Say what happened, what information is involved, and when. Do not try to investigate yourself, and do not delete evidence.

### 6.3 What the registered manager does

- Contains it — for example, recalls an email, asks the wrong recipient to delete a message and confirm it, locks or wipes a device, changes passwords, or removes access.
- Assesses the risk to the people whose information is involved: what information, how sensitive, how many people, and what could happen to them.
- Records it in our breach log, with the facts, the effects and what was done, whether or not it needs reporting.
- Decides whether it must be reported. If the breach is likely to result in a risk to people's rights and freedoms, it is reported to the Information Commissioner's Office without undue delay and within 72 hours of our becoming aware of it, through the Data Security and Protection Toolkit's incident reporting tool or directly to the Information Commissioner's Office.
- Tells the people affected without undue delay if the breach is likely to result in a high risk to them, explaining what happened and what they can do.
- Considers whether it is also a safeguarding matter, needs reporting to the police, or must be reported to commissioners under our contracts.
- Looks at why it happened and what will stop it happening again, and shares the learning.

A cyber attack that stops us accessing care records is also a business continuity incident. Follow our Business Continuity and Emergency Planning policy to keep care going.

## 7. Starters, movers and leavers

Access to systems is set up for a new starter on their first day, matched to their role, and changed when their role changes. When someone leaves, the office removes their access to every system, including their login to the site, by the end of their last day. Company devices, keys and any paper records are returned before they go.

## 8. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — leads on data security, completes the Data Security and Protection Toolkit, handles breaches, and decides on reporting.
- **Nominated individual** — makes sure the company provides the resources needed to keep information secure.
- **All staff** — follow this policy, complete training, and report breaches and suspicious messages at once.

## 9. Training

All staff complete data security training at induction and every year, as the Data Security and Protection Toolkit expects. Staff who handle more sensitive information, or administer systems, receive further training.

## 10. Records

We keep the breach log, the Data Security and Protection Toolkit submission and evidence, a list of who has access to which systems, and a register of company devices.
