## 1. Purpose

Flexible working can help people stay in care work around their families, health and other commitments, and helps {{organisation}} keep good staff. This policy explains how to ask for a change to your hours, times or place of work, and how we deal with your request fairly and in good time.

## 2. Scope

It applies to all employees from their first day of employment. Agency workers have separate, more limited rights through their agency.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Employment Rights Act 1996, as amended by the Employment Relations (Flexible Working) Act 2023 — the statutory right to request flexible working, from the first day of employment.
- Flexible Working Regulations 2014, as amended — who may request and how many times.
- Acas Code of Practice on requests for flexible working — the standard we are judged against.
- Equality Act 2010 — a refusal must not discriminate, for example against women with childcare responsibilities, or against disabled employees who need a change as a reasonable adjustment.

## 4. What you can ask for

You can ask to change:

- the hours you work — for example fewer hours, or a set number of hours;
- the times you work — for example only school hours, no weekends, or no early mornings;
- where you work — for example, for office staff, some days at home; for care staff, visits in a particular area.

Examples include part-time work, compressed hours, term-time working, job sharing, staggered start and finish times, and phased retirement.

## 5. Our policy

- We consider every request seriously and with an open mind.
- You can make up to two statutory requests in any 12 months.
- We decide each request, including any appeal, within two months of receiving it, unless you agree to a longer time.
- We do not refuse a request without first talking to you about it.
- We refuse a request only for one of the business reasons the law allows, and we explain why that reason applies.
- You will not be treated less favourably for asking.

Informal changes — swapping a shift, or a short-term change to your visits — can be agreed with the office at any time and do not need a statutory request.

## 6. How to make a request

Put your request in writing, by letter or email, and date it. Say:

- that it is a statutory request for flexible working;
- the change you are asking for, and when you would like it to start;
- whether you have made a request before, and when.

You do not have to say why you are asking, or how the change might affect the business, but it often helps us to find a solution if you do. If your request relates to a disability, tell us, so that we can also consider it as a reasonable adjustment.

## 7. How we deal with it

### 7.1 Considering the request

A manager acknowledges your request and looks at it carefully: what it would mean for the people you support, for continuity of care, for the rota and for your colleagues, and whether it, or something close to it, could work.

### 7.2 Talking to you

Before refusing a request, or agreeing to only part of it, the manager meets you to discuss it. You may bring a colleague or trade union representative. Together you look at whether the change could work, or whether something else would — a different pattern, a trial period, or a change at a later date.

### 7.3 The decision

The manager tells you the decision in writing. If the request is accepted, the letter says what the change is, when it starts, and whether it is permanent or a trial. A permanent change is a change to your contract, and you will not have a right to go back to your old pattern unless we agree it.

If the request is refused, the letter says which of the business reasons applies, explains why, and tells you how to appeal. The only reasons the law allows are:

- the burden of additional costs;
- a detrimental effect on our ability to meet customer demand — for us, the needs of the people we support;
- an inability to reorganise work among existing staff;
- an inability to recruit additional staff;
- a detrimental impact on quality;
- a detrimental impact on performance;
- insufficient work during the periods you propose to work;
- planned structural changes.

In care work the reasons that most often apply are the needs of the people we support — for example visits that must happen at set times, continuity of carers, or the need for enough staff at weekends and in the evenings. Even then, we look for what we can offer.

### 7.4 Trial periods

Where we are unsure whether a change will work, we may agree a trial period first, with a date to review it. The trial and the review are agreed in writing, and the decision period is extended by agreement.

### 7.5 Appeal

If your request is refused, you may appeal in writing within the time given in the letter, saying why. A different manager, where possible, considers the appeal and may meet you. The appeal decision is given in writing, and the whole process, including the appeal, is completed within the two months unless you agree to extend it.

### 7.6 Withdrawn requests

If you miss two meetings to discuss your request, or an appeal meeting, without good reason, we may treat the request as withdrawn and will tell you so in writing.

## 8. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure requests are dealt with fairly, consistently and within the time limits, and hears appeals or appoints someone to.
- **Managers** — consider requests with an open mind, meet employees before refusing, and record decisions and reasons.
- **Employees** — make requests in writing with the details needed, and take part in discussions.

## 9. Records

Requests, meeting notes, decisions and appeals are kept on the employee's file in the HR pack. An agreed change is confirmed as a change to the employee's terms. Records are kept for six years after employment ends.
