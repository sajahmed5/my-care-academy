## 1. Purpose

The people we support are often frail, and their health can change quickly. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} makes sure staff can give first aid, recognise when someone is becoming unwell, and get the right help quickly — while respecting the decisions people have made about the treatment they do and do not want.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works for us, in people's homes, in care homes and in our office. It covers first aid for the people we support, staff and visitors; medical emergencies; and recognising and acting on deterioration in health.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Safety (First-Aid) Regulations 1981, and the Health and Safety Executive's guidance on them — employers provide adequate first-aid equipment, facilities and trained people for their employees, based on an assessment of first-aid needs.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment — includes responding to changes in people's health and working with other services.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — advance decisions to refuse treatment, and best interests decisions for people who lack capacity.
- Resuscitation Council UK guidelines — basic life support and the management of choking.
- NHS guidance on recognising sepsis and other serious illness.

## 4. Our policy

- Every care worker and member of care staff is trained in basic life support and first aid before working alone or unsupervised.
- If in doubt, get help. It is always better to call for advice and not need it.
- We respect people's decisions about treatment, including DNACPR decisions, ReSPECT plans and advance decisions, and make sure staff know where to find them.
- We record every emergency and every significant change in someone's health, and learn from them.

## 5. First-aid arrangements

{{registered_manager}} carries out a first-aid needs assessment for our office and every place we work, and reviews it every year. From it:

- In home care, each care worker carries gloves, a face shield for rescue breaths, and a small first-aid kit, and the office has a first-aid kit and a trained first aider or appointed person. Care workers work alone, so every care worker is trained to give first aid.
- In a care home, there is at least one trained first aider on every shift, day and night, and first-aid kits are kept in places everyone knows. The kits are checked every month and restocked.

> Name your first aiders or appointed persons, and say where first-aid kits and any defibrillator are kept.

## 6. What to do in an emergency

### 6.1 When to call 999

Call 999 when someone:

- is not breathing, or not breathing normally;
- is unconscious or cannot be woken;
- has chest pain, or signs of a stroke — face drooping, arm weakness, slurred speech;
- has severe difficulty breathing, or is choking and it does not clear;
- has a seizure lasting longer than their care plan says is usual, or a first seizure;
- is bleeding heavily, or has a serious injury, such as a suspected broken hip or a head injury with drowsiness or vomiting;
- shows signs of sepsis (see 7.2).

Stay with the person. Give the call handler the address, the person's age, what has happened and any conditions you know about. They will tell you what to do, including where the nearest public defibrillator is. Then tell the office or on-call (in home care) or the shift leader (in a care home), and the person's family as the care plan says.

### 6.2 When to call 111 or the GP

Call 111 or the person's GP when they are unwell and you are worried, but it is not an emergency — for example a high temperature, a fall without obvious injury, a urine infection, or a gradual change in how they are. Follow the advice you are given and record it.

### 6.3 Cardiac arrest

If someone is unresponsive and not breathing normally, call 999 and start CPR, unless there is a valid DNACPR decision, ReSPECT plan or advance decision that says CPR should not be attempted, or it is clear that the person has been dead for some time. If there is a DNACPR decision, tell the call handler, show it to the paramedics, and stay with the person. If you are not sure whether a DNACPR decision exists, or cannot find it, start CPR.

### 6.4 Choking

If the person can cough, encourage them to keep coughing. If the cough is not working, give up to five back blows, then up to five abdominal thrusts, as you have been trained. Call 999 if the blockage does not clear. If the person becomes unresponsive, start CPR. Anyone who has had abdominal thrusts must be checked by a doctor afterwards. Report every choking episode, so that the person's eating and drinking can be reviewed (see Nutrition and Hydration).

### 6.5 After an emergency

Record what happened through the site's concerns process the same day, as set out in Accidents, Incidents and Near Misses. Tell the office or the shift leader if you used anything from your first-aid kit, so it can be replaced. A medical emergency can be upsetting; talk to your manager about any support you need.

## 7. Recognising deterioration

### 7.1 Knowing what is normal for the person

The people who know someone best are often the first to see that something is wrong. Watch for changes from what is usual for that person: being more confused or sleepy, eating or drinking less, passing less urine, new breathlessness, a change in skin colour, falls, or simply "not themselves". Record what you notice, and tell the office or the shift leader the same day. In a care home, where staff are trained to, take and record observations such as temperature, pulse and breathing rate, and use the structured approach the home has adopted to describe changes when calling for advice.

### 7.2 Sepsis

Sepsis is a life-threatening reaction to an infection. Call 999 if a person with a possible infection has any of these:

- slurred speech, new confusion or drowsiness;
- extreme shivering or muscle pain;
- passing no urine in a day;
- severe breathlessness, or breathing very fast;
- skin that is mottled, bluish, pale or blotchy, or a rash that does not fade when a glass is rolled over it;
- says they feel as if they are going to die.

Call 111 or the GP the same day if a person seems to have an infection and is getting worse.

### 7.3 Conditions in the care plan

Some people have conditions — diabetes, epilepsy, heart failure, chronic lung disease — where the care plan sets out the signs to look for and what to do. Follow it. If what you see is not in the care plan, get advice.

## 8. Decisions about treatment

- **DNACPR** (do not attempt cardiopulmonary resuscitation) — a decision by the person's doctor or senior clinician, ideally made with the person, that CPR should not be attempted. It applies only to CPR; all other care and treatment continue.
- **ReSPECT** — a plan made with the person that records their wishes and the clinical recommendations for emergency care and treatment.
- **Advance decision to refuse treatment** — a decision the person made while they had capacity. If it is valid and applies to the situation, it is legally binding.

The registered manager makes sure these documents are recorded in the care plan, that staff know where the original is kept — in the person's home, or with their records in the care home — and that they go with the person if they are taken to hospital. If you are told about one of these decisions but cannot see the document, tell the office or the shift leader at once so that it can be checked. See End of Life Care, Death and Advance Decisions.

## 9. If someone has died

If you find a person who appears to have died, and their death was expected and planned for, follow their end of life care plan and contact the GP or the community nurse. If it was not expected, call 999. Do not move the person or anything around them unless you are told to. Care staff do not confirm death, unless they have been trained and authorised to verify an expected death under local arrangements. Tell the office or the shift leader straight away.

## 10. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — assesses first-aid needs, makes sure there are enough first aiders, and makes sure treatment decisions are in care plans.
- **Office, on-call staff and shift leaders** — give advice, contact families and professionals, and make sure emergencies are recorded.
- **All staff** — act quickly in an emergency and report changes in health.

## 11. Training

All care staff complete basic life support and first aid during induction, as part of the Care Certificate, and refresh it every year. Training covers choking, recognising deterioration and sepsis, and how to find and follow DNACPR and ReSPECT decisions. First aiders hold a current first-aid at work qualification. Training is recorded on the training matrix.

## 12. Records

Emergencies are recorded through the concerns process and in the person's daily notes, and changes in health and the advice received in their care record. First-aid needs assessments and kit checks are kept by the registered manager. All are kept for the periods set in Record Keeping and Retention.
