## 1. Purpose

Everyone we support and everyone who works for us has the right to be treated fairly, as an individual, with their human rights respected. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} prevents discrimination, values difference, and upholds human rights in the care we give in people's homes and in how we treat our staff.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works for us, including agency staff and volunteers, and to job applicants. It covers how we treat clients and their families, how we treat staff from recruitment to leaving, and how clients and families treat our staff.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Equality Act 2010 — protects people from discrimination because of the nine protected characteristics: age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage and civil partnership, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sex, and sexual orientation. Marriage and civil partnership is protected at work, not in the services we provide. The Act also requires reasonable adjustments for disabled people.
- Equality Act 2010, as amended by the Worker Protection (Amendment of Equality Act 2010) Act 2023 and the Employment Rights Act 2025 — since 30 October 2026, employers must take all reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment of their employees, and are liable for harassment of their employees by people who are not employees, such as clients and their families, unless they took all reasonable steps to prevent it.
- Human Rights Act 1998 — public authorities must act compatibly with the rights in the European Convention on Human Rights. Under section 73 of the Care Act 2014, when we provide care that a local authority has arranged or paid for, in whole or in part, we are treated as a public authority for that care.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 10: Dignity and respect — care must respect people's privacy, dignity, independence and autonomy, and have due regard to their protected characteristics. Regulation 13: Safeguarding service users from abuse and improper treatment — care must not be given in a way that discriminates against a person because of a protected characteristic.
- Accessible Information Standard — information and communication support for people with a disability or sensory loss (see Accessible Information and Communication).
- CQC's Single Assessment Framework — the quality statements "Equity in access" and "Equity in experiences and outcomes" under responsive, and "Workforce equality, diversity and inclusion" under well-led. CQC looks at human rights through the FREDA principles: Fairness, Respect, Equality, Dignity and Autonomy.

## 4. What we mean

- Direct discrimination — treating someone less favourably because of a protected characteristic, such as refusing a care package because the client is gay, or a job because the applicant is pregnant. It includes a characteristic someone is thought to have, or that of someone they are linked to, such as a disabled child.
- Indirect discrimination — a rule or practice that applies to everyone but puts people with a protected characteristic at a disadvantage, and cannot be justified as a proportionate way of meeting a legitimate aim. A rule that every care worker must work every Sunday morning could be one.
- Discrimination arising from disability — treating a disabled person unfavourably because of something connected with their disability, such as absence, unless it can be justified.
- Harassment — unwanted conduct related to a protected characteristic, or of a sexual nature, that violates someone's dignity or creates an intimidating, hostile, degrading, humiliating or offensive environment for them (see Bullying and Harassment).
- Victimisation — treating someone badly because they complained of discrimination, or helped someone else to.
- Reasonable adjustments — changes that remove or reduce a disadvantage a disabled person faces, such as information in another format, a different visit time, or adapted equipment. For services, we think ahead rather than waiting to be asked.

## 5. Our policy

- We provide care to anyone whose needs we can meet, whatever their protected characteristics.
- We treat everyone we support as if the Human Rights Act applied to their care, whoever pays for it.
- We plan care around each person's identity, culture, faith, language and way of life, and never assume.
- We recruit, pay, train and promote staff fairly, on merit, and make reasonable adjustments for disabled staff and applicants.
- We challenge discrimination wherever it comes from, including from clients and families towards our staff.

## 6. Human rights in everyday care

The rights in the Convention that matter most in homecare are:

- Article 2, the right to life — we act on deterioration, call for help, and do not withhold treatment or food and drink because of someone's age or disability.
- Article 3, freedom from inhuman or degrading treatment — no one is left in soiled clothes or bedding, handled roughly, or neglected.
- Article 5, the right to liberty — care that restricts a person's freedom must be lawful. In a person's own home, a deprivation of liberty needs an order of the Court of Protection (see Deprivation of Liberty).
- Article 8, respect for private and family life, home and correspondence — the client's home is theirs; we respect their routines, relationships, privacy and choices, including choices we would not make ourselves.
- Article 9, freedom of thought, conscience and religion — people practise their faith as they wish.
- Article 10, freedom of expression — people can say what they think, including complaints.
- Article 14 — these rights apply to everyone without discrimination.

## 7. What to do

### 7.1 Planning care

At assessment and at every review, ask the client what matters to them: their name and pronouns, language, faith and practices, diet, dress, washing customs, festivals, relationships and how they want to be cared for at the end of life. Record it in the care plan and follow it. If a client prays at set times or fasts, plan visit times and medicines with them and their GP.

Clients may ask for a care worker of the same sex for intimate care. We respect this, as the care plan records, and plan rotas to meet it.

### 7.2 Communication

Ask how each person communicates and meet that need, using a professional interpreter for important conversations. Our Accessible Information and Communication policy sets out how.

### 7.3 If a client or family discriminates against you

Some clients or families may refuse a care worker, or abuse them, because of their race, religion, sexual orientation or another characteristic. We do not change a care worker because of their race or another protected characteristic, as that would be discrimination against our own staff. A request about the sex of the care worker for intimate care is different, and is met as 7.1 says.

If it happens, leave the client safe, and leave the home if you feel unsafe. Tell the office or on-call the same day, and record it through the site's concerns process. The manager speaks to the client or family, makes clear that it must stop, and agrees a plan with you. Where illness such as dementia is a cause, we review the care plan and support you. Where a crime may have been committed, we support you to report it to the police. As a last resort, we may end the service (see Ending a Service). You will not be blamed for reporting it, or made to go back alone.

### 7.4 If you see discrimination

If you see or hear anyone treat a client or colleague unfairly because of who they are, challenge it if it is safe, and report it to your manager. Discrimination that harms a client may also be abuse, and is reported under our Safeguarding policy. You can also use our Whistleblowing policy.

### 7.5 At work

- Requests for time off for religious festivals, or to change shifts for prayer or fasting, are considered fairly and agreed where the rota allows.
- Disabled staff can ask for reasonable adjustments at any time, such as changes to rounds, equipment or hours. We may also ask Access to Work, the government scheme, for support.
- Complaints of discrimination are dealt with under our Grievance or Bullying and Harassment policy.

## 8. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — leads on equality and human rights, makes sure assessments and care plans reflect people's identity, deals with discrimination by anyone, and makes reasonable adjustments.
- **Nominated individual** — reviews what complaints, concerns, staff feedback and workforce information show about fairness, every year, and makes sure action is taken.
- **Office staff and rota planners** — plan visits around clients' needs and staff adjustments, and record concerns.
- **Care workers** — treat every client as an individual, follow their care plan, and challenge and report discrimination.

## 9. Training

All staff complete equality, diversity and human rights training at induction, as part of the Care Certificate for those new to care, and refresh it every year. Managers are also trained in reasonable adjustments and in preventing harassment.

## 10. Records

Each client's identity, culture, faith and communication needs are recorded in their care plan. Discrimination incidents are recorded through the concerns process, and staff complaints and adjustments in the HR pack. Equality monitoring information is kept separately and confidentially.
