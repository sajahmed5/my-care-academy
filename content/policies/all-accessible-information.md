## 1. Purpose

People cannot make choices about their care, agree to it, or tell us when something is wrong unless we communicate in a way that works for them. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} finds out how each person communicates, records it, shares it, and meets it — including large print, easy read, British Sign Language, interpreters, and the everyday support of hearing aids and glasses.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone we support, and to their family carers and representatives where they have a disability or sensory loss that affects how they receive information from us. It applies to everyone who works for us and to every kind of information: letters, care plans, contracts, menus, notices, conversations and phone calls.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Accessible Information Standard, issued by NHS England under section 250 of the Health and Social Care Act 2012 — providers of publicly funded adult social care must identify, record, flag, share and meet the information and communication needs of people with a disability, impairment or sensory loss.
- Equality Act 2010 — the duty to make reasonable adjustments, including providing information in an accessible format and auxiliary aids such as interpreters.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 9: Person-centred care, and Regulation 11: Need for consent — people are involved in their care and give consent based on information they can understand.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — all practicable steps, including communication support, must be taken to help a person make a decision before concluding they cannot.
- Health and Care Act 2022 — registered providers must make sure staff receive training on learning disability and autism, including how to communicate, appropriate to their role.

## 4. Our policy

- We apply the Accessible Information Standard to everyone we support, whether their care is publicly funded or not.
- We ask every person how they prefer to communicate and receive information, and we do not assume.
- We provide information in the format the person needs, without charge.
- We use professional interpreters for important conversations. We do not rely on children to interpret, and we use family members only for everyday matters when the person chooses.
- We keep hearing aids, glasses and communication aids working, because they are part of how a person communicates.

## 5. What to do

### 5.1 Identify

At assessment, ask the person, and those who know them, how they communicate and what helps: their first language, whether they use British Sign Language or another sign system, whether they need large print, braille, easy read, audio, pictures or objects of reference, and whether they need more time, a quieter place, or someone to support them. Ask the same of family carers who have their own needs.

### 5.2 Record

Record the person's needs clearly in their care plan: what the need is, how to meet it, and what does not work. Be specific — "Uses a hearing aid in the right ear; face him and speak clearly; do not shout; write down anything important" is more use than "hard of hearing".

### 5.3 Flag

Make the need visible to anyone who reads the person's record, so that every care worker, office worker or shift leader sees it before they contact the person. Office staff check it before they send a letter or phone.

### 5.4 Share

With the person's agreement, or where the law allows, tell other services about their communication needs: the GP, the hospital, the district nurse, the local authority, ambulance crews. Make sure a hospital passport or similar summary goes with the person to hospital.

### 5.5 Meet

Provide information in the agreed format, and support every conversation the way the care plan says. In practice:

- face the person, in good light, and get their attention before you speak;
- use short sentences and plain words; check they have understood, without testing them;
- use gestures, pictures, writing or objects where they help;
- give the person time to answer;
- make sure hearing aids are in, switched on and working, and glasses are clean and their own;
- for people living with dementia, a learning disability or autism, follow their communication profile;
- for people who are deafblind, follow the method recorded in their plan, such as hands-on signing.

### 5.6 Interpreters and translation

For assessments, reviews, consent to treatment, best interests decisions, complaints, safeguarding and any conversation where misunderstanding could cause harm, use a professional interpreter, in person, by video or by phone. Book British Sign Language interpreters as early as you can. Written translations of key documents are arranged by the office.

> Name the interpreting and translation service you use, how staff book it, and how it is paid for.

### 5.7 Hearing aids and glasses

Care staff help people clean and check their hearing aids and glasses as their care plan says, change hearing aid batteries or charge them, and report faults or lost items to the office or the shift leader the same day. Glasses are labelled where the person agrees. Ask the GP to refer the person for a hearing test, or arrange a sight test, when you notice their hearing or sight getting worse; sight tests can be done at home for people who cannot get to an optician.

### 5.8 Our own information

Our guide to the service, contracts, complaints leaflet and care plans are available in large print and easy read, and in other formats and languages on request. Notices in a care home are at eye level, in a clear typeface, with pictures where they help — for example on menus and activity boards.

### 5.9 When things change

Communication needs change with illness, a stroke, a new diagnosis or deterioration. Report changes so the care plan can be updated, and ask whether a speech and language therapist or sensory services should be involved.

## 6. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure the Standard is applied, that interpreters and accessible formats are available, and that the policy is reviewed.
- **Seniors, shift leaders and office staff** — identify and record needs at assessment and review, flag and share them, and arrange formats and interpreters.
- **All staff** — read and follow each person's communication needs, look after their aids, and report changes.

## 7. Training

All staff learn about communication, sensory loss and the Accessible Information Standard in induction, as part of the Care Certificate, and refresh it every year. Staff complete training on learning disability and autism appropriate to their role. Staff who support people with specific needs — sign language, deafblindness, aphasia — receive further training.

## 8. Records

Communication needs are recorded in each person's care plan and reviewed with it. Interpreter bookings and documents provided in other formats are noted on the person's record.
