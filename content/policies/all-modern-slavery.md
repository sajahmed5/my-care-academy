## 1. Purpose

Modern slavery is the exploitation of people through force, threats, deception or debt for someone else's gain. Social care is a sector where it has been found, particularly among workers recruited from overseas. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} makes sure that no one who works for us, or for those we buy from, is exploited, and how our staff recognise and report modern slavery in the people we support or in the homes and communities they visit.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works for us, to everyone involved in recruiting staff, and to the agencies and suppliers we use.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Modern Slavery Act 2015 — makes slavery, servitude, forced or compulsory labour, and human trafficking criminal offences. Section 54 requires commercial organisations with a turnover above the threshold set by regulations to publish an annual slavery and human trafficking statement; it does not apply to every company.
- Care Act 2014 — modern slavery is one of the types of abuse and neglect covered by the local authority's adult safeguarding duties.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 13: Safeguarding service users from abuse and improper treatment.
- National Minimum Wage Act 1998 and its regulations — workers are paid at least the minimum wage for all the time they work, including time travelling between visits.
- Conduct of Employment Agencies and Employment Businesses Regulations 2003 — UK agencies may not charge workers a fee for finding them work.
- Home Office guidance for sponsors of skilled workers — what a sponsor must do, and the costs it must not pass on to the worker.

## 4. Our policy

- We do not tolerate modern slavery or labour exploitation in our business or in our supply chain.
- We never charge anyone a fee for a job, never hold anyone's passport, identity documents or bank cards, and never make work conditional on living in accommodation we provide.
- Everyone is paid directly into a bank account in their own name, for all the hours they work.
- A concern about modern slavery is a safeguarding concern, reported the same day.

## 5. In our own recruitment

Workers recruited from overseas can arrive owing large debts to agents in their home country, or be told they must repay costs if they leave. We protect against this:

- We recruit directly or through reputable agencies that do not charge workers fees, and we ask agencies to confirm this in writing.
- At interview and again at induction, we ask every new worker, in private, whether they paid anyone to get this job, whether anyone holds their documents, and whether they owe money to anyone connected with the job. We explain that the answer will not affect their job.
- We pay the sponsorship costs the Home Office says a sponsor must not pass on, and never take them back through wages or a repayment clause.
- We check right to work in person and see original documents, as our Right to Work and Sponsored Workers policy sets out, and the worker keeps their own documents.
- We look out for warning signs: several workers giving the same address, phone number or bank account; someone else always answering for the worker, bringing them to work or collecting their pay; a worker who seems afraid, or who cannot say where they live.
- Pay is checked so that travel time between visits, training and waiting time are paid, and deductions are only those the law and the contract allow.

## 6. In our supply chain

We expect the agencies, cleaning and laundry contractors, food suppliers and other businesses we use to follow the law on modern slavery and minimum wage. Before using a staffing agency, the registered manager checks that it pays its workers properly and does not charge them fees. If we have reason to think a supplier is exploiting its workers, we raise it with the supplier and, if necessary, report it and stop using them.

## 7. Recognising modern slavery

Our care workers and care staff may see signs of modern slavery in a person we support, in someone living in or visiting the household, or in someone working there. Signs include someone who:

- seems controlled by another person, is rarely alone, or lets someone else speak for them;
- has no access to their own passport, identity documents or money;
- works very long hours for little or no pay, or is working to repay a debt that never seems to go down;
- lives in poor or overcrowded conditions, or sleeps where they work;
- seems frightened, withdrawn, or unwilling to talk about their situation;
- has injuries they cannot or will not explain.

A person we support may also be exploited — for example, a person whose home is being used by others for criminal activity (sometimes called cuckooing), or a person who is being made to work or hand over their benefits. Some people do not see themselves as victims, and may be afraid of the authorities.

## 8. What to do

### 8.1 If someone is in immediate danger

Call 999.

### 8.2 Reporting a concern

- Do not confront anyone you suspect, and do not try to rescue the person yourself.
- If the person talks to you, listen, do not make promises you cannot keep, and do not press them for details.
- Write down what you saw and heard, with the date and time.
- Tell {{registered_manager}} the same day on {{office_phone}}, or through the on-call line out of hours, and record it through the site's concerns process as a safeguarding concern.

You can also call the Modern Slavery and Exploitation Helpline on 08000 121 700, at any time, for advice or to report a concern. If the concern is about a member of our own management, use our Whistleblowing policy or call the helpline.

### 8.3 What the registered manager does

The registered manager:

- refers the concern to the local authority's adult safeguarding team, or to children's social care where the person is under 18, and to the police;
- notifies CQC where the concern involves a person we support (see Reportable Incidents);
- for a concern within our own workforce, makes sure the worker is safe, keeps them in work where they want to stay, and takes advice from the police and the helpline before acting.

The National Referral Mechanism is the UK's system for identifying victims of modern slavery and making sure they get support. Only designated first responders — including local authorities, the police and some charities — can make a referral to it, so we do not refer directly; we pass our concern to the local authority or police, who decide whether to refer. An adult must give their consent to a National Referral Mechanism referral; a child does not need to. Whatever the person decides, they remain entitled to a safeguarding response.

## 9. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — leads on modern slavery, oversees recruitment checks, checks agencies and suppliers, and makes referrals.
- **Nominated individual** — makes sure our recruitment and pay practices meet this policy, and that a statement is published if the law requires one.
- **Anyone who recruits or pays staff** — follows the checks in this policy and reports warning signs.
- **All staff** — recognise and report concerns the same day.

## 10. Training

All staff are made aware of modern slavery as part of safeguarding training at induction, refreshed every year. Staff who recruit are trained in the warning signs and in our recruitment checks.

## 11. Records

Concerns and referrals are kept through the concerns process. Recruitment checks, including the answers new workers give about fees and debts, are kept on their staff file. Agency and supplier checks are kept with our contracts. All are kept for the periods set in Record Keeping and Retention.
