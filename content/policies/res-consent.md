## 1. Purpose

Living in a care home does not mean handing over decisions about your body, your room or your day. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} asks for consent before giving care, respects residents who say no or change their mind, and acts lawfully when a resident cannot consent for themselves.

## 2. Scope

It applies to every resident, to everyone who works in the home, including agency staff, and to everything we do with or for a resident: personal care, medicines, moving and handling, checks at night, photographs, monitoring and sharing information.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 11: Need for consent — care and treatment are given only with the person's consent or, where a person aged 16 or over lacks capacity, in line with the Mental Capacity Act 2005.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 and its Code of Practice — the five principles, assessing capacity, best interests decisions, lasting powers of attorney, deputies and advance decisions.
- Human Rights Act 1998, Article 8 — the right to respect for private life, including choices about one's own body.
- UK GDPR, the Data Protection Act 2018 and the common law duty of confidentiality — when we need consent to use or share information.
- CQC's Single Assessment Framework — the quality statement on consent to care and treatment, under the key question "effective".

## 4. Our policy

- We assume every resident can make their own decisions unless it is shown, for a particular decision, that they cannot.
- We ask before every task, every time. Consent given once is not consent for ever.
- A resident with capacity may refuse any care or treatment, or withdraw consent at any time, even if we think it unwise. We respect it.
- Nobody consents for a resident who has capacity. Relatives, however close, cannot consent for a resident unless they hold a lasting power of attorney or deputyship covering that decision.

## 5. What valid consent is

Consent is valid when the resident has capacity to make the decision, has the information they need in a way they understand, and agrees freely, without pressure from anyone. It can be given in different ways:

- Spoken — the resident says yes when you explain what you would like to do.
- Written — the resident signs or marks a record. We ask for it for photographs used for activities or publicity. A signature does not make consent valid on its own; understanding and agreement do.
- Implied — the resident shows agreement by what they do, such as holding out their arm for a dressing or standing when you offer to help them up. Silence, or simply going along with care, is not consent. If you are not sure, ask.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 When a resident moves in

In the first weeks, the senior or key worker agrees the care plan with the resident, recording their decisions, how they like to be asked, any lasting power of attorney, deputy or advance decision, who they want kept informed, and anything they do not want shared. Where their capacity to agree to moving here is in doubt, see Assessment and Starting a Service.

### 6.2 Every time you give care

- Knock and wait before entering a resident's room. It is their home.
- Explain what you would like to do and why, and ask. Use the resident's preferred language, simple words, gestures or pictures, as the care plan describes.
- In a lounge or dining room, ask quietly and privately. Do not discuss personal care or medicines in front of other residents or visitors.
- At night, residents still need to agree to checks, repositioning and continence care. The care plan records what the resident has agreed to at night — for example, whether they want to be woken — and you follow it.
- Watch for signs that the resident is no longer happy, and stop.

### 6.3 When a resident says no or changes their mind

- Stop. Never force, trick or rush a resident into care.
- Ask what is wrong. Offer another time, another way or another member of staff.
- If the refusal could cause harm — a missed medicine, a fall, pressure damage, not eating — explain the risk honestly and tell the shift leader straight away.
- Record what you offered, what the resident said and what you did.
- If a resident keeps refusing, the senior reviews the care plan with them and records an informed refusal as their choice (see Choice and Positive Risk-Taking). Refused medicines are reported to the prescriber.
- If you think someone is pressuring a resident to agree or refuse, tell the shift leader the same day. It may be a safeguarding concern.

### 6.4 When a resident may not be able to consent

Capacity is about one decision at one time. If you think a resident cannot understand, remember, weigh up or communicate a decision, tell the shift leader. A trained senior or the registered manager assesses capacity for that decision and, if the resident lacks it, a best interests decision is made with the people who know them, choosing the least restrictive option (see Mental Capacity, Best Interests and Advocacy). You must still explain what you are doing and involve the resident as far as they can be.

### 6.5 Attorneys, deputies and advance decisions

An attorney under a lasting power of attorney for health and welfare may make the care decisions it covers, but only once the resident lacks capacity to make them, and may refuse life-sustaining treatment only if the document says so. A lasting power of attorney for property and financial affairs gives no authority over care. A deputy appointed by the Court of Protection may make only the decisions the order sets out. The registered manager checks the document or the Office of the Public Guardian register and keeps a note on file.

A valid advance decision to refuse treatment that applies to the situation must be followed. Other wishes made in advance are not binding, but are weighed in every best interests decision. See End of Life Care, Death and Advance Decisions.

### 6.6 Health care and vaccinations

The GP, nurse or other professional who treats a resident, including when giving vaccinations in the home, seeks consent to that treatment. Staff tell them what they know about the resident's capacity, wishes and any attorney or advance decision. Staff do not sign consent forms for residents, and families are not asked to consent unless they hold authority to do so.

### 6.7 Photographs, monitoring and information

- Photographs for care, such as of a wound, are taken only with consent or in the resident's best interests, on a company device. Photographs for activities or publicity need written consent (see Social Media, Phones and Technology).
- Sensor mats, door alarms and cameras in a resident's room need the resident's consent or a best interests decision, and are a restriction to be kept under review (see Restraint and Restrictive Practice and CCTV).
- We share information with the professionals involved in a resident's care, and the resident is told this when they move in. We share with family and friends only what the resident agrees to, or what is in their best interests if they lack capacity (see Confidentiality).

### 6.8 Residents aged 16 or 17

We care for adults. If we are asked to support a person aged 16 or 17, the registered manager takes advice first: they can consent to their own care, but cannot make a lasting power of attorney or advance decision, and the Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards do not apply to them.

### 6.9 Deprivation of liberty

Where a resident lacks capacity to consent to living here and to their care, and that care may amount to a deprivation of liberty, the Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards apply and the registered manager requests an authorisation. Since the Supreme Court's judgment in A Reference by the Attorney General for Northern Ireland (June 2026), which overruled the 2014 Cheshire West judgment, and the Department of Health and Social Care's guidance on it, this depends on the resident's whole situation, including whether they object. See Deprivation of Liberty.

## 7. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure consent is sought and recorded, capacity is assessed where it is in doubt, attorneys' and deputies' authority is checked, and DoLS authorisations are requested where needed.
- **Shift leaders** — make sure staff on their shift follow residents' decisions, act on refusals that could cause harm, and pass concerns about capacity to the registered manager.
- **Seniors and key workers** — agree care plans with residents and review them when a resident keeps refusing.
- **Care staff** — ask before every task, respect refusals, record them, and report concerns.

## 8. Training

All staff learn about consent and the Mental Capacity Act during induction, as part of the Care Certificate, and refresh it every year. Staff who assess capacity complete further training first. Training is recorded on the training matrix.

## 9. Records

Care plan agreements, refusals, capacity assessments, best interests decisions, notes of attorneys, deputies and advance decisions, and consent for photographs and sharing information are kept on the resident's record for as long as our retention schedule requires (see Record Keeping and Retention).
