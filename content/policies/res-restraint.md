## 1. Purpose

Restraint is not only holding someone. A keypad on the front door, a sensor mat, a bed rail, a lap belt or medicine given to calm someone are all ways of restricting a resident. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} keeps restrictions to the minimum, uses them only when the law allows, and makes sure each one is planned, recorded and reviewed.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works in the home and to every resident. It covers restrictions on one resident, restrictions that affect everyone living here, and restrictions visitors ask for or put in place.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Mental Capacity Act 2005, section 6 — restraint means using or threatening force to make a person do something they resist, or restricting their liberty of movement whether or not they resist. For a person who lacks capacity it is lawful only if the person using it reasonably believes it is necessary to prevent harm to them, and it is a proportionate response to the likelihood and seriousness of that harm.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 13: Safeguarding service users from abuse and improper treatment — control or restraint that is not necessary to prevent harm, or not proportionate to it, is not allowed.
- Human Rights Act 1998 — freedom from inhuman or degrading treatment (Article 3), the right to liberty (Article 5) and the right to respect for private life and home (Article 8).
- Mental Capacity Act 2005, Schedule A1 — the Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards, under which restrictions that amount to a deprivation of liberty in a care home must be authorised.
- Department of Health, Positive and Proactive Care: reducing the need for restrictive interventions (2014).
- Restraint Reduction Network Training Standards.
- Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency guidance on the safe use of bed rails.

## 4. What counts as restraint

- Physical — holding or guiding a resident against their will, or blocking their way.
- Mechanical — equipment that limits movement: bed rails, lap belts and wheelchair belts, tables fixed across a chair, or a reclining or tilted chair the resident cannot get out of.
- Chemical — medicine given mainly to control behaviour, including "when required" sedatives.
- Environmental — keypads and locked doors, locked kitchens, gardens or lifts, or moving a walking frame out of reach.
- Surveillance — sensor mats, door alarms, bed sensors and cameras.
- Psychological — telling a resident they are not allowed to do something, threats, or withholding something they want until they comply.

Some of these are part of good care when a resident with capacity agrees — for example, a resident who asks for bed rails, or for a sensor mat so staff come when they get up at night. They become restraint when the resident does not agree or cannot.

## 5. Our policy

- We prevent the need for restraint by understanding residents and supporting them well (see Positive Behaviour Support).
- Any restriction must be necessary to prevent harm, proportionate to that harm, the least restrictive option, and for the shortest time.
- We do not use blanket restrictions — rules applied to every resident because some need them — unless each is assessed and justified, and residents who do not need it are not held back by it.
- Staff do not use planned physical restraint unless it is in the resident's plan and they have been trained in the specific technique to the Restraint Reduction Network Training Standards.
- We never use restraint as punishment, for convenience, because there are not enough staff, or because a family asks us to without proper assessment.
- We never use face-down restraint, techniques that cause pain, or seclusion — shutting a resident alone in a room or space they cannot leave.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 Planned restrictions

When a restriction is being considered, a senior or the registered manager:

- checks first whether there is a less restrictive way of meeting the need — for example, a low bed and crash mat instead of bed rails, or a walk with staff instead of a locked door;
- assesses the resident's capacity to agree to it, and if they lack capacity, makes a best interests decision with family, any attorney and the professionals involved;
- for bed rails, belts and specialist seating, makes sure a suitably trained person has assessed the resident and the equipment, and that bed rails fit the bed and mattress without gaps that could trap them (see Equipment and Maintenance);
- writes it into the care plan: what it is, why it is needed, when it is used, including at night, how staff use it, and when it will be reviewed;
- considers whether, with everything else in the resident's care, it may amount to a deprivation of liberty.

### 6.2 Doors, keypads and shared spaces

The front door and any other secured doors keep residents at risk safe, but they restrict everyone. Residents who can safely come and go are told the code or let out whenever they ask. Locks on kitchens, sluices and stores are for safety, and residents can still have drinks and snacks when they want them. The registered manager reviews every restriction that affects all residents at least every year, and when the residents living here change.

### 6.3 Using an agreed restriction

Use the restriction exactly as the care plan says and no more. Check equipment before each use. Explain what you are doing to the resident every time. Record its use where the plan asks you to. Night staff check residents with bed rails or belts as often as the plan says. If the resident is objecting or distressed, or the restriction no longer seems right, stop if it is safe to, and tell the shift leader.

### 6.4 In an emergency

If you need to act to prevent serious and immediate harm — stopping a resident walking into the road, or separating two residents who are fighting — call for help, use the least force for the shortest time, then let go. Afterwards:

- check everyone involved for injury, and get medical help if needed;
- tell the shift leader straight away;
- record what happened, why you acted, what you did and for how long, as an incident through the site's concerns process, before the end of your shift.

### 6.5 Restrictions by visitors

If a visitor restricts a resident — holding them, keeping them in their room, giving them something to make them sleep — or asks staff to, do not take part, and tell the shift leader the same day. The registered manager talks with the visitor and refers to safeguarding where needed.

### 6.6 Medicines

Medicine used to calm a resident is given only as prescribed, with a clear plan for when a "when required" dose may be given, and only after the plan's other steps have been tried. Each dose is recorded with the reason. Medicine is never given to make a resident sleep or sit still for staff convenience, including at night. Hiding medicine in food or drink without the resident's knowledge is covert medication and is done only as our Covert Medication policy allows.

### 6.7 Deprivation of liberty

Restraint, a resident who objects or tries to leave, and covert or calming medicine all make it more likely that a resident is deprived of their liberty. Where that may be so, the registered manager requests a Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards authorisation, includes the restrictions in the request, and makes sure staff follow any conditions. See Deprivation of Liberty.

## 7. After any restriction is used

After any emergency restraint, the shift leader talks with the staff involved, and with the resident when they are ready, about what could prevent it next time. The registered manager reviews every incident, decides whether it must be referred to safeguarding or notified to CQC, and makes sure the care plan is updated.

## 8. Reviewing and reducing

Each planned restriction is reviewed at the date set in the care plan, after any incident, and when the resident's needs change. The registered manager keeps a list of restrictions in use across the home, reviews it regularly with the incident records to find any that can be reduced or stopped, and reports through our governance meetings.

## 9. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure restrictions are lawful, assessed, recorded and reviewed; reviews incidents; requests DoLS authorisations; makes referrals and notifications; and leads on reducing restrictive practice.
- **Seniors** — assess restrictions and write them into care plans.
- **Shift leaders** — make sure restrictions on their shift are used as planned, lead the response to emergencies, and debrief staff.
- **Care staff** — use only the restrictions in the care plan, report and record every use outside it, and report restrictions by visitors.

## 10. Training

All care staff learn about restraint, restrictive practice, the Mental Capacity Act and the Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards during induction and refresh it every year. Staff who use bed rails, belts or specialist seating are trained to check and use them. Any training in physical intervention is certified against the Restraint Reduction Network Training Standards and given to staff who support a resident whose plan includes it. Training is recorded on the training matrix.

## 11. Records

Assessments, best interests decisions, care plan entries for each restriction, records of use and reviews are kept on the resident's record, and incidents through the concerns process, for as long as our retention schedule requires.
