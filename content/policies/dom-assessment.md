## 1. Purpose

The first few days of a new care package are when things are most likely to go wrong: a visit missed because the key safe code was not passed on, a medicine given that the client had stopped, a care worker arriving who does not know the client cannot stand. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} decides whether we can meet a person's needs, how we assess them before care starts, and how we make the first visits safe and welcoming.

## 2. Scope

It applies to every new client, however the referral reaches us: from a local authority, an integrated care board, a hospital discharge team, a family or the person themselves. It also applies when a client returns to us after a hospital stay or a long break, and when their needs change so much that the care plan must be rewritten.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 9: Person-centred care — care is designed with the person, after an assessment of their needs and preferences.
- Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment — risks to the person are assessed, and we do all that is reasonably practicable to reduce them.
- Regulation 18: Staffing — we accept care only when we have enough suitably skilled staff to provide it.
- Care Quality Commission (Registration) Regulations 2009, Regulation 19: Fees — people who pay for their care are given a written statement of the terms and the fees.
- Care Act 2014 — the local authority's assessment and care and support plan, and the wellbeing principle we share with it.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — the person is assumed to be able to agree to their own care unless shown otherwise.
- Consumer Rights Act 2015 — our terms with people who pay for themselves must be fair and clear, and our service given with reasonable care and skill.
- NICE guideline NG21, Home care: delivering personal care and practical support to older people living in their own homes.

## 4. Our policy

- We only agree to provide care that we can provide safely, at the times the person needs it, with staff who have the right skills. Saying no, or not yet, is better than a package we cannot cover.
- Nobody receives a visit until we have assessed them ourselves. A referral or another organisation's assessment informs ours; it does not replace it.
- The person is at the centre of the assessment. We speak with them, not only about them, and involve the people they want involved.
- The first care worker to visit knows what they need to know before they arrive.

## 5. What to do

### 5.1 Taking the referral

The office records the referral: who the person is, where they live, what care is asked for and when, who is funding it, the referrer's contact details, and anything urgent such as a hospital discharge date. The registered manager or a senior decides, the same day where possible, whether we have the capacity in that area and at those times. If we do not, we say so promptly so that the referrer can look elsewhere.

### 5.2 Information before the visit

We ask the referrer for what they hold: the local authority's assessment and support plan, the hospital discharge summary, a current list of medicines, known risks, and the details of any lasting power of attorney. We read it before the assessment visit.

### 5.3 The assessment visit

A trained senior or the registered manager visits the person at home before care starts. Where a hospital discharge makes that impossible, they assess the person on the ward or by phone with the ward and family, and visit at home on the first day of care. The assessment covers:

- what the person wants from their care, what matters to them, and how they like things done;
- their health, mobility, personal care, eating and drinking, continence, skin and memory;
- the support they need with medicines, and who orders and collects them;
- how they communicate, and any sensory loss or language need;
- their capacity to agree to the care, and any attorney, deputy or advance decision;
- family and other carers, and what each of them will do;
- the home: access, the key safe or other way in, lighting, pets, smoking, equipment, and anything that puts a lone care worker at risk;
- moving and handling, and whether one or two care workers are needed;
- cultural, religious and dietary needs, and any preference about the gender of care workers.

If the person cannot agree to the assessment or to the care, follow our Mental Capacity, Best Interests and Advocacy policy.

### 5.4 Deciding and agreeing

After the visit, the assessor decides with the registered manager whether we can meet the person's needs. If we can, we agree with the person the visit times, the length of each visit, and what will be done on each one. If the times the person needs do not fit the time commissioned, we tell the commissioner rather than shorten the visit.

People who pay for their own care receive our terms and conditions and a written statement of the fees before care starts, explained in a way they understand. Where the local authority pays, the person is still told what we will do, when, and how to contact us or complain.

### 5.5 Before the first visit

Before the first visit the office makes sure that:

- the care plan and risk assessments are written, even if some parts will be added in the first weeks;
- the medicines support is agreed and recorded, with the medicines record ready if we are to give medicines;
- equipment needed for safe moving and handling is in place, or the visit is planned around its absence;
- the way into the home is recorded securely, as our Key Holding and Key Safes policy sets out;
- the visits are on the rota with care workers who have the skills the person needs, and, where possible, the same small team.

### 5.6 The first visits

Where we can, the senior who assessed the person goes on the first visit, or introduces the first care worker. On the first visit, the care worker:

- reads the care plan before going in;
- introduces themselves, shows their ID, and asks how the person likes to be addressed;
- follows the care plan, asking the person at each step;
- checks that what they find matches the plan — the medicines, the equipment, the person's mobility — and phones the office if it does not;
- writes a full record of the visit.

The office phones the person or their family after the first few visits to ask how it is going.

### 5.7 Early review

A senior reviews the care plan with the person after the first few weeks, or sooner if the person's needs are not what we expected. Many people coming home from hospital improve quickly; their care should shrink as they regain independence, and we tell the commissioner when it does.

## 6. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — decides whether we can accept each package, makes sure assessments are done by trained staff, and checks that no care starts without an assessment and a care plan.
- **Senior or care coordinator** — carries out assessments, writes the first care plan and risk assessments, and reviews them in the early weeks.
- **Office staff** — record referrals, set up the rota and access details, and follow up the first visits.
- **Care workers** — read the plan before the first visit and report anything that does not match it.

## 7. Training

Staff who carry out assessments are trained in assessment and care planning, risk assessment, the Mental Capacity Act and moving and handling assessment before they assess anyone alone, and are observed doing their first assessments. Their competence is recorded on their file.

## 8. Records

The referral, the assessment, the agreed care plan, risk assessments, signed terms or the commissioner's order, and any capacity assessment are kept on the client's record for as long as we keep it (see Record Keeping and Retention). Referrals we decline are recorded with the reason.
