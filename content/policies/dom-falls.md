## 1. Purpose

Falls are one of the most common reasons older people lose their independence or go into hospital, and many of our clients live alone with nobody to help them up. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} helps clients reduce their risk of falling without restricting their lives, and exactly what care workers do when they find a client on the floor.

## 2. Scope

It applies to every client and every care worker, office and on-call worker. It covers falls during a visit, clients found on the floor when we arrive, and falls clients tell us about afterwards.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment — risks are assessed and reduced, and we respond properly when someone is hurt.
- Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and the Manual Handling Operations Regulations 1992 — care workers must not be put at risk by lifting a person who has fallen.
- Lifting Operations and Lifting Equipment Regulations 1998 — lifting equipment is suitable, maintained and used only by trained staff.
- NICE guidance on assessing and preventing falls in older people, and NICE guideline NG232, Head injury: assessment and early management.
- Care Quality Commission (Registration) Regulations 2009, Regulation 18 — notification of serious injuries to people while we are providing their care.
- Reporting of Injuries, Diseases and Dangerous Occurrences Regulations 2013 (RIDDOR) — some injuries arising from our work activity must be reported to the Health and Safety Executive.

## 4. Our policy

- We assess every client's risk of falling and agree with them how to reduce it, respecting their right to stay active and take reasonable risks.
- Care workers never lift a fallen client by hand.
- A client who may be injured is not moved, and gets medical help.
- Every fall is recorded and reviewed, and the client's risks are reassessed.

## 5. Reducing the risk

### 5.1 Assessment

At assessment and at each review, a senior asks about falls in the past year, and looks at the things that make falls more likely: problems with walking or balance, dizziness on standing, poor eyesight, foot problems, continence and night-time toilet trips, confusion, alcohol, and medicines such as sleeping tablets, blood pressure tablets and some antidepressants. They look at the home: loose rugs, trailing wires, clutter, poor lighting, stairs, and whether the client can reach what they need. The risks and the agreed actions are written in the care plan.

### 5.2 Actions we take

With the client's agreement, we:

- ask the GP for a falls referral, a medicines review, or a sight or hearing check where needed;
- ask for an occupational therapy assessment for equipment such as grab rails, raised toilet seats or a perching stool;
- suggest a telecare pendant or falls detector so the client can call for help between visits;
- suggest a home fire and safety visit where the fire service offers one;
- help the client choose footwear that fits and grips.

### 5.3 On every visit

- Leave the walking aid, the phone, the pendant and a drink within reach.
- Leave lights on where the client wants them, especially for night-time trips to the toilet.
- Clear trip hazards you can safely move, with the client's agreement, and report others.
- Encourage the client to stand slowly and wait a moment before walking.
- Report any change: new unsteadiness, dizziness, confusion, a near miss, or the client saying they have fallen.

## 6. What to do if you find a client on the floor

### 6.1 Stop and check

- Check it is safe for you to go in and approach. Speak to the client calmly and tell them you are there to help.
- Check whether they respond, and whether they are breathing normally. If they are not, phone 999, follow the call handler's instructions and start CPR if trained, unless a valid do not attempt cardiopulmonary resuscitation decision is in place.
- Do not move them yet.

### 6.2 Phone 999 if

- they are unresponsive, very drowsy or confused beyond their usual;
- they have hit their head and take blood-thinning medicine, or are vomiting, or have a wound to the head;
- they have pain in the neck, back, hip or leg, or a limb looks shortened, turned or deformed;
- they have chest pain, difficulty breathing, or a new weakness on one side or slurred speech;
- they are bleeding heavily;
- they have been on the floor for a long time or are very cold;
- you are in any doubt.

Stay with the client, keep them warm with a blanket, and do not give them food or drink. Phone on-call once the ambulance has been called.

### 6.3 If the client is not injured

If the client says they are not hurt, has no signs of injury, and none of the reasons to call 999 apply:

- let them rest for a few minutes, then ask whether they feel able to get up themselves;
- if they can, talk them through getting up in stages — rolling onto their side, getting onto hands and knees, and using a sturdy chair to rise — without taking their weight;
- if they cannot get up themselves, do not lift them. Phone on-call, who will arrange help: a falls response service, the telecare provider's responder, a second care worker trained in lifting equipment kept in the home, or 111 or 999 for advice or an ambulance;
- while you wait, keep them warm and comfortable on the floor, with a pillow and a blanket.

Only care workers who are trained in it may use lifting equipment that is kept in the client's home, and only when the client has been checked and is uninjured.

> List any local falls response service, telecare responder or community falls pick-up service in your area, with how to contact them.

### 6.4 After the client is up

- Check them for injuries again and ask how they feel. Watch for pain, dizziness, drowsiness or confusion, which may appear later — especially after a blow to the head.
- Do not leave until they are settled and safe, or until help has arrived. Tell on-call if this will make you late for your next visit, so they can tell the next client.
- With the client's agreement, tell their family and the GP.
- If they hit their head, or later develop any of the signs in 6.2, phone 999 or 111.

### 6.5 Falls you hear about later

If the client tells you they fell between visits, or you see signs that they did — new bruises, things knocked over, the client sleeping in a chair — ask how they are, check for injuries, and report it to on-call the same day.

## 7. After a fall

Record what happened, what you found, what you did and who you told, in the daily notes and through the site's concerns process as an incident, the same day. A senior reviews the client's falls risk assessment and care plan within days, and makes referrals. The registered manager reviews every fall, decides whether it must be notified to CQC as a serious injury during our care, whether it is reportable under RIDDOR because it arose from our work activity, and whether the duty of candour applies. Repeated falls, or falls where care may not have been given as planned, are considered for safeguarding.

## 8. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure falls risks are assessed and plans are in place, reviews every fall, and makes notifications.
- **Seniors** — assess falls risk, write care plans, make referrals, and review after a fall.
- **Office and on-call staff** — support care workers at the scene, arrange help, rearrange visits, and contact families.
- **Care workers** — reduce hazards, follow this procedure, and record and report every fall.

## 9. Training

All care workers learn falls prevention, what to do after a fall and safe moving and handling during induction, and refresh it every year. First aid training covers head injuries and resuscitation. Care workers who use lifting equipment are trained and assessed on that equipment, and their competence is recorded on their file.

## 10. Records

Falls risk assessments, care plans, incident records, reviews and referrals are kept on the client's record, and incidents through the concerns process, for as long as our retention schedule requires.
