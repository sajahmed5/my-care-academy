## 1. Purpose

Everyone takes risks in daily life: crossing a road, having a drink, cooking a meal, living alone. Needing care does not take away a person's right to do the same. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} supports people to make their own choices, including choices that carry risk, while doing what we reasonably can to keep them and others safe.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works for us and to every person we support, in their own home or in a care home. It covers everyday choices and the bigger decisions that carry a real risk of harm.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — a person who can make a decision may make one that others think unwise, and anything done for a person who cannot decide must be in their best interests and the least restrictive option.
- Care Act 2014 — the wellbeing principle: the person is best placed to judge their own wellbeing, and their views, wishes and control over their day-to-day life matter.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 9: Person-centred care, and Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment — risks are assessed and we do all that is reasonably practicable to reduce them.
- Human Rights Act 1998 — the right to respect for private life includes the right to make choices about how one lives.
- Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 — we must also protect staff and others from risks to their health and safety.

## 4. Our policy

- People have the right to make their own choices about their lives. Our job is to inform and support, not to decide for them.
- We assess risks with the person, not for them, and weigh the benefits of what they want to do alongside the possible harm.
- We look for ways to make a choice safer before we consider saying it cannot happen.
- We respect a decision made by a person who has the capacity to make it, even if we think it unwise.
- Where a person cannot make a particular decision, we decide in their best interests, choosing the least restrictive option.
- We do not accept a risk that would seriously harm other people or staff, and we always act on safeguarding concerns.

## 5. What positive risk-taking looks like

Positive risk-taking means agreeing, with the person and the people who know them, how they can do something that matters to them as safely as reasonably possible. For example:

- a client who wants to keep walking to the shop on their own, with a phone, a pendant alarm and an agreed route;
- a resident who wants to keep making their own hot drinks, with a kettle tipper and a check of how they manage;
- a person with swallowing difficulties who chooses to eat food the speech and language therapist has advised against, knowing the risks;
- a person who smokes, with a fire safety check, a safe place to smoke, and an understanding of the fire risk from emollient creams on clothes and bedding;
- a person who wants to keep drinking alcohol, or go out to see friends at night.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 When a choice carries a risk

If a person wants to do something that you think may put them at risk, do not simply stop them. Listen to why it matters to them. Tell them honestly what worries you. If it is safe to go ahead today, do so; if it is not, tell the office, on-call or the shift leader straight away. Record the conversation in their daily notes.

### 6.2 Assessing the risk

A senior, the shift leader or the registered manager meets the person — and, if they wish, their family and the professionals who know them — to talk the choice through. Together they consider:

- what the person wants to do and why it matters to them;
- what could go wrong, how likely it is, and how serious it would be;
- what the person loses if they cannot do it — independence, pleasure, confidence, contact with others;
- what could make it safer, and who would do what;
- whether the risk falls only on the person or also on others, such as other residents, neighbours or staff;
- whether the person has the capacity to make this decision.

### 6.3 Recording the agreement

The outcome is recorded in the person's care plan as a risk assessment: the choice, the risks discussed, the steps agreed to reduce them, who is responsible for each, and when it will be reviewed. It records that the person was given the information, in a way they understood, and what they decided. Where the person accepts a risk against advice, the care plan says so plainly, so that staff know they are supporting a lawful choice and are not at fault for doing so.

### 6.4 When capacity is in doubt

If you think the person may not understand the risk, tell your manager. A capacity assessment for that decision is carried out and recorded (see Mental Capacity, Best Interests and Advocacy). If the person lacks capacity for the decision, a best interests decision is made with the people who know them. It still takes account of what the person wants and has valued, and it still looks for the least restrictive way. Restricting someone is not automatically the safe option: it carries harms of its own.

### 6.5 When the risk is to others

A person's right to choose does not extend to putting others in serious danger. If a choice puts other people we support, visitors, neighbours or staff at serious risk, the registered manager decides what must change, with the person and, where needed, the local authority, the fire service or the police. A person's right to choose also does not mean leaving staff in unsafe working conditions.

### 6.6 When it may be neglect or abuse

A pattern of choices that leaves a person seriously at risk — not eating, not taking life-saving medicine, refusing all help, living in conditions that endanger their health — may be self-neglect. A choice may also not be truly free if someone is pressuring the person. In either case, report it to your manager the same day; it may need a safeguarding referral (see Safeguarding).

### 6.7 Reviewing

Risk agreements are reviewed at the date set, and sooner if something goes wrong, the person's health changes, or they change their mind. A fall or an incident does not automatically mean the agreement was wrong; we look at what happened and agree with the person what, if anything, should change.

## 7. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure risks are assessed with people rather than for them, signs off risk agreements where the possible harm is serious, and decides where a choice cannot be supported.
- **Seniors and shift leaders** — lead the conversations and write the risk assessments.
- **All staff** — respect people's choices, support the agreed plan, and report changes and concerns.

## 8. Training

All staff learn about choice, the Mental Capacity Act and risk in induction, as part of the Care Certificate, and refresh it every year. Staff who write risk assessments are trained in person-centred risk assessment before they do so.

## 9. Records

Risk assessments, risk agreements, capacity assessments and best interests decisions are kept on the person's care record and kept for as long as our retention schedule requires (see Record Keeping and Retention).
