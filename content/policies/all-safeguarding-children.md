## 1. Purpose

{{organisation}} supports adults, but our staff meet children every day: grandchildren visiting, children living in a client's home, young carers, and the children of our own staff. This policy makes sure that anyone who works for us recognises when a child may be at risk of harm and knows exactly what to do about it.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works for us, in every place we work. A child is anyone under 18.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Children Act 1989 and Children Act 2004 — the local authority's duty to investigate when a child may be suffering significant harm, and every agency's duty to co-operate.
- Working Together to Safeguard Children — the statutory guidance on how organisations work together to protect children.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 13: Safeguarding service users from abuse and improper treatment.
- Data Protection Act 2018 — allows us to share information without consent where it is needed to protect a child.
- Local safeguarding children partnership procedures for the areas we work in.

## 4. Our policy

- The welfare of the child comes first. A concern about a child is never ignored because they are not the person we are there to support.
- Everyone has a duty to report. It is not your job to prove abuse or to investigate, only to pass on what you have seen or heard, straight away.
- We never promise to keep a child's disclosure secret.
- We work with children's social care and the police, and share what they need.

## 5. Recognising abuse and neglect

There are four main kinds of abuse of children:

- Physical abuse — hitting, shaking, burning, poisoning, or any other physical harm.
- Emotional abuse — persistent ill-treatment that harms a child's emotional development, including seeing or hearing domestic abuse.
- Sexual abuse — involving a child in sexual activity, including online and through images.
- Neglect — persistent failure to meet a child's basic needs: food, warmth, clothing, safety, supervision or medical care.

Children can also be harmed through criminal or sexual exploitation, female genital mutilation, radicalisation, forced marriage and trafficking.

Signs to look out for include unexplained injuries, a child who is frightened of an adult, a child left alone or in charge of an adult's care beyond their age, a home without food or heating, a child who is often absent from school during the day, or a child who tells you something worrying. A young carer looking after a parent or grandparent is not in itself a concern, but they may need support, and the local authority can assess them.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 If a child is in immediate danger

Call 999. Then tell the office straight away.

### 6.2 If a child tells you something

- Stay calm, listen, and let them talk. Do not ask leading questions or press for detail.
- Tell them they were right to tell you, and that you will need to tell someone who can help. Do not promise to keep it secret.
- Do not confront the person the child has named.
- As soon as you can, write down what the child said in their own words, what you saw, the date and time, and who was there.

### 6.3 Reporting

Report every concern to {{registered_manager}} the same day, on {{office_phone}} or through the out-of-hours on-call line, and record it through the site's concerns process as a safeguarding concern. If the registered manager is not available, or the concern is about them, report it directly to children's social care.

The registered manager decides on the same day whether to refer to the local authority's children's social care team, and makes the referral by phone, followed in writing. If in doubt, they ask children's social care for advice. Where we work in more than one area, the referral goes to the area where the child lives.

> List the children's social care front door (the multi-agency safeguarding hub or equivalent) and out-of-hours number for each local authority you work in.

### 6.4 After a referral

We record the referral, the name of the person we spoke to, and what was agreed. We co-operate with any enquiry, give information when asked, and do not tell the family about the referral if children's social care or the police ask us not to. Staff are supported after a distressing disclosure.

## 7. Allegations against staff

If anyone who works for us is alleged to have harmed a child, or behaved in a way that suggests they may be unsuitable to work with children, the registered manager tells the Local Authority Designated Officer (LADO) within one working day and follows their advice. We may suspend the person while it is looked into, as a precaution. We refer to the Disclosure and Barring Service anyone we dismiss, or who leaves, because they harmed or posed a risk of harm to a child or adult, as the law requires.

## 8. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — leads on safeguarding children, decides on and makes referrals, liaises with the LADO, and keeps this policy and the contact numbers up to date.
- **Office and on-call staff** — take reports seriously, pass them to the registered manager at once, and refer directly if they cannot reach them.
- **All staff** — recognise and report concerns the same day, and record what they saw and heard.

## 9. Training

All staff complete safeguarding training that covers children during induction and refresh it every year. The registered manager and anyone who covers for them complete higher-level safeguarding training.

## 10. Records

Concerns, referrals and their outcomes are recorded through the concerns process, kept securely, and shared only with those who need to know. Records about a child are kept for as long as our record retention schedule requires.
