## 1. Purpose

Dignity is how people feel when they are treated as the individuals they are: listened to, in control of their own lives, and not exposed or made to feel small. This policy sets out what {{organisation}} expects of everyone who works for us, so that the people we support are treated with dignity, privacy and respect in every moment of their care, including the small ones.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works for us, in every place we provide care, and to how we speak about and record the people we support as well as how we treat them in person.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 10: Dignity and respect — people are treated with dignity and respect, their privacy is respected, their independence and involvement in the community are supported, and equality characteristics are taken into account.
- Regulation 9: Person-centred care — care reflects the person's needs and preferences.
- Human Rights Act 1998 — in particular the right not to be subjected to degrading treatment (Article 3) and the right to respect for private and family life, home and correspondence (Article 8).
- Equality Act 2010 — people must not be discriminated against because of age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage or civil partnership, pregnancy or maternity, race, religion or belief, sex or sexual orientation.
- Care Act 2014 — the wellbeing principle, which includes personal dignity and control over day-to-day life.
- Data Protection Act 2018 and UK GDPR — personal information is kept private.

## 4. Our policy

- We treat each person as an adult with their own history, views and rights, never as a task.
- People choose how they are addressed, how they look, what they wear, when they get up and go to bed, and how their care is given.
- Privacy is protected during personal care, in conversations and in records.
- We help people do as much as they can for themselves.
- We do not tolerate disrespect, discrimination, rough handling or humiliation from anyone, and we act on it.

## 5. What to do

### 5.1 How you speak

- Use the name the person has asked to be called. Do not use "love", "darling" or "sweetie" unless they have told you they like it.
- Speak to the person, not over them or about them to someone else in the room — including to other staff.
- Explain what you are about to do and ask before you do it, every time.
- Speak in a way they understand, at their pace, at their level, without talking down to them.
- Do not talk about other people you support, your colleagues or your own problems in front of them.

### 5.2 Personal care

- Make sure the door is closed and curtains or blinds are drawn before you start.
- Keep the person covered as much as possible, uncovering only the part you are washing.
- Let them do what they can for themselves, even if it takes longer.
- Respect their wishes about the gender of the person who helps them with intimate care, as their care plan records.
- Make sure they are dressed in their own clean clothes, as they choose, with their hair, glasses, hearing aids, teeth and jewellery as they like them.
- Deal with continence care quickly and discreetly.

### 5.3 Where they live

In a person's own home, you are a guest. Knock or ring and wait, even if you have a key or a key safe code; call out as you go in. Ask before you open drawers or cupboards, and follow the house rules — shoes, pets, smoking, phones. Do not use their phone, toilet, food or drink without permission.

In a care home, each resident's room is their private space. Knock and wait for an answer before you go in. Residents may lock their room and have a key where it is safe, keep their own belongings, and decorate their room as they wish. Their post is theirs to open. They can see visitors in private. In shared rooms, lounges and dining rooms, protect privacy with screens and by having private conversations elsewhere.

### 5.4 Mealtimes

People eat what they choose, where they choose, at their own pace. Help people to eat by sitting beside them, at their eye level, giving them time. Offer aprons or napkins, not bibs, unless they want one. Do not mix courses or medicine into food without a care plan that says so.

### 5.5 Culture, faith and identity

Find out from the person and their care plan what matters to them: religious practices, prayer times, washing and dietary rules, how they dress, and how they want to be cared for at the end of their life. Respect people's sexual orientation and gender identity, and their relationships. Never assume.

### 5.6 Privacy of information

Do not discuss the people we support where you can be overheard, in personal messages or on social media. Do not take photographs of them on your own phone. Write records as if the person will read them — in plain, respectful language, describing what happened rather than labelling. "She refused to wash" is less useful than "She said she did not want a wash this morning, and we agreed I would ask again at lunch."

### 5.7 People who cannot speak up for themselves

People living with dementia, a learning disability or a serious illness are most at risk of being treated without dignity, often without anyone noticing. Take more care, not less. Watch for how they react, use what their care plan says helps them, and involve family and advocates.

### 5.8 When you see something wrong

If you see or hear anyone treat a person without dignity — a rough word, ignoring a call for help, leaving someone exposed or soiled, laughing at them — step in if it is safe, and report it the same day to your manager. If it amounts to abuse or neglect, follow our Safeguarding policy. You can also use our Whistleblowing policy. You will be supported for speaking up.

## 6. Hearing from the people we support

We ask the people we support and their families how they feel they are treated: at reviews, in surveys, and through spot checks and conversations. Concerns and complaints about dignity are taken seriously and recorded through the site's concerns process. We look for patterns and change what we do.

## 7. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — sets the standard, checks it through observation, spot checks and feedback, and acts on every concern.
- **Seniors, shift leaders and office staff** — model respectful care, challenge poor practice on the spot, and record concerns.
- **All staff** — follow this policy in every interaction, and report anything that falls short.

## 8. Training

All staff learn about dignity, privacy, equality and person-centred care in induction, as part of the Care Certificate, and refresh it every year. Spot checks and supervision include how staff treat people, and are recorded on each person's file.

## 9. Records

Care plans record each person's preferences about how they are addressed, their personal care, their culture and faith, and who they want involved. Concerns about dignity, and what we did about them, are recorded through the concerns process.
