## 1. Purpose

A care home is a shared home, where residents of different backgrounds, faiths and identities live side by side, cared for by a team just as varied. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} prevents discrimination, values difference, and upholds human rights in the home, so that every resident can live as themselves and every member of staff is treated fairly.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works in the home, including agency staff and volunteers, and to job applicants. It covers how we treat residents and their visitors, how residents treat each other, how we treat staff from recruitment to leaving, and how residents and visitors treat our staff.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Equality Act 2010 — protection from discrimination because of the nine protected characteristics: age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage and civil partnership, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sex, and sexual orientation (marriage and civil partnership at work only, not in the services we provide); and the duty to make reasonable adjustments for disabled people.
- Equality Act 2010, as amended by the Worker Protection (Amendment of Equality Act 2010) Act 2023 and the Employment Rights Act 2025 — since 30 October 2026, employers must take all reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment of their employees, and are liable for harassment of their employees by people who are not employees, such as residents and visitors, unless they took all reasonable steps to prevent it.
- Human Rights Act 1998 — public authorities must act compatibly with the European Convention on Human Rights. Under section 73 of the Care Act 2014, when we provide care and accommodation that a local authority has arranged or paid for, in whole or in part, we are treated as a public authority for that care.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 10: Dignity and respect — care respects people's privacy, dignity and autonomy, with due regard to their protected characteristics; and Regulation 13: Safeguarding service users from abuse and improper treatment — care must not discriminate because of a protected characteristic.
- Accessible Information Standard — information and communication support for people with a disability or sensory loss (see Accessible Information and Communication).
- CQC's Single Assessment Framework — the quality statements "Equity in access" and "Equity in experiences and outcomes" under responsive, and "Workforce equality, diversity and inclusion" under well-led. CQC looks at human rights through the FREDA principles: Fairness, Respect, Equality, Dignity and Autonomy.

## 4. What we mean

- Direct discrimination — treating someone less favourably because of a protected characteristic, such as refusing an admission because of a person's religion, or a job because the applicant is pregnant. It includes a characteristic someone is thought to have, or that of someone they are linked to.
- Indirect discrimination — a rule or practice that applies to everyone but puts people with a protected characteristic at a disadvantage, and cannot be justified as a proportionate way of meeting a legitimate aim; for example, a fixed menu that never meets halal, kosher or vegetarian needs.
- Harassment — unwanted conduct related to a protected characteristic, or of a sexual nature, that violates someone's dignity or creates an intimidating, hostile, degrading, humiliating or offensive environment for them (see Bullying and Harassment).
- Victimisation — treating someone badly because they complained of discrimination, or helped someone else to.
- Reasonable adjustments — changes that reduce a disadvantage a disabled person faces, such as picture signs, a ramp, a hearing loop or a different routine. We think ahead rather than waiting to be asked.

## 5. Our policy

- We consider every admission on the person's needs and whether we can meet them, whatever their protected characteristics.
- We treat every resident as if the Human Rights Act applied to their care, whoever pays for it.
- We run the home around residents' identities, cultures, faiths and ways of life, not one "normal" routine.
- We recruit, pay, train and promote staff fairly, on merit, and make reasonable adjustments for disabled staff and applicants.
- We challenge discrimination wherever it comes from: staff, residents, visitors or contractors.

## 6. Human rights in the home

The rights in the Convention that matter most in a care home are:

- Article 2, the right to life — we act on deterioration, call for help, and do not withhold treatment or food and drink because of someone's age or disability.
- Article 3, freedom from inhuman or degrading treatment — no one is left in soiled clothes or bedding, handled roughly, or left without help.
- Article 5, the right to liberty — care that restricts a resident's freedom may be a deprivation of liberty, which must be authorised under the Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards (see Deprivation of Liberty).
- Article 8, respect for private and family life, home and correspondence — a resident's room is their home, their post is theirs, and they choose their routines, relationships and visitors.
- Article 9, freedom of thought, conscience and religion — residents practise their faith, or none, as they wish.
- Article 14 — these rights apply to everyone without discrimination.

## 7. What to do

### 7.1 Living in the home

Before admission and at every review, ask the resident what matters to them: name and pronouns, language, faith, diet, dress, washing customs, festivals, relationships and wishes for the end of life. Record it in the care plan and follow it, day and night.

- Menus offer food that meets residents' religious, cultural and ethical diets, prepared as the diet requires, and the cook knows each resident's needs.
- Residents can attend worship, see a faith leader in private, have a quiet space to pray, keep objects of faith in their room and mark their own festivals. Celebrations reflect who lives here, and nobody has to join in.
- Lesbian, gay, bisexual and trans residents, and couples of any kind, are as welcome as anyone. Some older people have spent a lifetime hiding who they are; make it safe not to.
- Residents may ask for a care worker of the same sex for intimate care. We respect this, as the care plan records, and plan the rota to meet it, at night too.

### 7.2 Communication

Ask how each resident communicates and meet that need, using a professional interpreter for important conversations. Our Accessible Information and Communication policy sets out how.

### 7.3 When residents treat each other unfairly

If a resident makes racist, homophobic or other offensive remarks to another resident in a lounge or at the table, step in calmly, support the person affected, and tell the shift leader. Where illness such as dementia plays a part, the care plan is reviewed to reduce it. Repeated or serious incidents are recorded through the site's concerns process and may be safeguarding concerns.

### 7.4 If a resident or visitor discriminates against you

We do not take a member of staff off a resident's care because of their race or another protected characteristic, as that would be discrimination against our own staff. A request about the sex of the care worker for intimate care is different, and is met as 7.1 says.

If a resident or visitor abuses you because of who you are, keep the resident safe, step away if you need to, and tell the shift leader straight away, who records it through the concerns process. The manager makes clear to the resident or visitor that it must stop, and agrees a plan with you; where a visitor is responsible, this may include a restriction under our Visiting policy. Where illness is a cause, we review the care plan. Where a crime may have been committed, we support you to report it to the police. You will not be blamed for reporting it.

### 7.5 If you see discrimination

Challenge it if it is safe, and report it to the shift leader or manager. Discrimination that harms a resident may be abuse, reported under our Safeguarding policy; you can also use our Whistleblowing policy.

### 7.6 At work

- Requests for time off for religious festivals, or to change shifts for prayer or fasting, are considered fairly and agreed where the rota allows.
- Disabled staff can ask for reasonable adjustments at any time, and we may ask the government's Access to Work scheme for support.
- Complaints of discrimination are dealt with under our Grievance or Bullying and Harassment policy.

## 8. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — leads on equality and human rights, makes sure care plans, menus and activities reflect residents, deals with discrimination by anyone, and makes reasonable adjustments.
- **Nominated individual** — reviews what complaints, concerns, staff feedback and workforce information show about fairness, every year, and makes sure action is taken.
- **Shift leaders** — act on incidents on their shift, support those affected, and record concerns.
- **All staff** — treat every resident as an individual, follow their care plan, and challenge and report discrimination.

## 9. Training

All staff, including kitchen and domestic staff, complete equality, diversity and human rights training at induction, as part of the Care Certificate for those new to care, and refresh it every year. Managers are also trained in reasonable adjustments and in preventing harassment.

## 10. Records

Each resident's identity, culture, faith, diet and communication needs are recorded in their care plan. Discrimination incidents are recorded through the concerns process, and staff complaints and adjustments in the HR pack. Equality monitoring information is kept separately and confidentially.
