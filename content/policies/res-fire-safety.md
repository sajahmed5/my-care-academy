## 1. Purpose

In a care home fire, many residents cannot get out by themselves, some will not understand what is happening, and at night there are fewer staff to help them. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} prevents fires, how we make sure everyone can be moved to safety, and what every member of staff must do when the alarm sounds.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works in the home, on every shift, and to visitors, contractors and agency staff while they are in the building.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 — the "responsible person" (the employer, or whoever controls the premises) must carry out a suitable and sufficient fire risk assessment, put fire safety measures in place, plan for emergencies, maintain fire safety equipment, and train staff. Since amendments made by the Building Safety Act 2022, the whole fire risk assessment must be recorded, along with who carried it out.
- HM Government guidance, Fire safety risk assessment: residential care premises.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment, and Regulation 15: Premises and equipment.
- Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999 — emergency procedures and competent help.

The local fire and rescue service enforces the Fire Safety Order and may inspect the home. CQC also checks that residents are safe from fire.

## 4. Our policy

- {{organisation}} is the responsible person for the home, and {{registered_manager}} manages fire safety day to day.
- The fire risk assessment is carried out by a competent person, recorded in full, and reviewed every year and whenever anything significant changes.
- Every resident has a personal emergency evacuation plan.
- There are always enough trained staff on duty — day and night — to carry out the evacuation strategy.

## 5. The fire risk assessment

The fire risk assessment looks at what could start a fire, what could burn, who is at risk, and how they would escape or be protected. It sets out the home's evacuation strategy and the measures needed to make it work. It is reviewed every year, and straight away after a fire or near miss, when the building or its use changes, or when residents' dependency or the number of staff on duty — especially at night — changes significantly.

The actions it identifies are given a named person and a date, and the registered manager checks they are done.

## 6. Our evacuation strategy

Most care homes are divided into fire compartments: areas separated by fire-resisting walls and doors designed to hold back fire and smoke for a period of time. This allows progressive horizontal evacuation — moving residents away from the fire, through a fire door, into the next compartment on the same floor, where they are protected. From there, they can be moved further, or out of the building, if that becomes necessary. Residents in areas not affected by the fire may be safest staying where they are, in line with the strategy, until the fire and rescue service decide otherwise.

> Describe your home's evacuation strategy: its compartments and zones, which way residents move from each, and where the assembly point is.

## 7. Personal emergency evacuation plans

Every resident has a personal emergency evacuation plan (PEEP), completed when they move in and reviewed with their care plan and whenever their mobility, health or understanding changes. It records:

- how the resident can be moved — walking with help, in a wheelchair, on an evacuation mat or sledge, or in their bed;
- how many staff are needed, and what equipment;
- anything that affects how they will respond, such as hearing loss, dementia, medicines that make them drowsy, or oxygen use.

A summary of all residents' PEEPs, and a list of who is in which room, is kept by the fire panel and updated every day, so that staff and the fire and rescue service know who needs help.

## 8. If you discover a fire

- Raise the alarm at the nearest fire alarm call point.
- Call 999 and ask for the fire service, giving the home's address. Do not assume the alarm system has called them.
- Only tackle a small fire with an extinguisher or fire blanket if you have been trained, it is safe, and you have a clear way out.
- Move anyone in immediate danger out of the room and close the door behind you.

## 9. When the alarm sounds

- The shift leader goes to the fire panel and finds which zone the alarm is in.
- Staff go to the fire panel, or to the place agreed in the strategy, and take instructions from the shift leader.
- Staff check the zone the panel shows. Feel a door with the back of your hand first, and do not open it if it is hot.
- If there is a fire, move residents out of the affected compartment, starting with those closest to the fire, using their PEEPs. Close every door behind you.
- Check every room in the compartment, and close each door once it is clear.
- Do not use the lift, unless it is an evacuation lift and the strategy says so.
- The shift leader checks that everyone — residents, staff and visitors — is accounted for, using the room list and the signing-in book.
- When the fire service arrive, the shift leader tells them where the fire is, who has been moved, and who may still be inside, and gives them the PEEP summary.

Do not go back into an area that is on fire, or reset the alarm, until the fire service or a competent person says it is safe.

## 10. Night staffing

At night there are fewer staff on duty, and residents are asleep. The fire risk assessment must show that the evacuation strategy can be carried out with the number of staff on duty at night, and the rota must never fall below that number. Night staff know the strategy and every resident's PEEP. If the number of night staff falls, or residents' needs increase, the registered manager reviews the fire risk assessment straight away.

## 11. Fire drills

Every member of staff takes part in a fire drill at least twice a year. Drills are held at different times of day, and some are run with the number of staff who would be on duty at night, so that we know the strategy works when it is hardest. Drills practise moving people using a volunteer or a mannequin, not a resident. Each drill is timed and recorded, with what went well and what needs to improve.

## 12. Preventing fire

- Smoking is allowed only in the places the home has designated for it, and never in shared areas indoors. Residents who smoke have an individual risk assessment, which may include supervision, flame-retardant aprons or bedding, and storing lighters and cigarettes safely.
- Emollient creams soak into clothing and bedding and make them burn more easily. Residents who use them have this noted in their care plan, and their bedding and clothing are changed and washed regularly.
- Residents who use oxygen have a risk assessment; oxygen is stored safely, and no one smokes or uses a naked flame near it. The fire and rescue service is told where oxygen is stored.
- Residents' own electrical items are checked before use. Charging of phones, e-cigarettes and mobility scooters is done safely, as the risk assessment says.
- Kitchens and laundries are kept free of grease and lint, and rubbish is removed regularly and kept away from the building.
- Fire doors are never wedged or propped open. Doors fitted with hold-open devices release when the alarm sounds.
- Escape routes are kept clear at all times.

## 13. Checks and maintenance

The fire safety log records every check:

- the fire alarm tested every week from a different call point, and serviced by a competent engineer;
- emergency lighting tested every month, and given a full test every year;
- fire doors, self-closers and hold-open devices checked regularly;
- fire extinguishers and fire blankets checked monthly and serviced every year;
- escape routes and fire exits checked every day.

Faults are put right straight away.

## 14. Roles and responsibilities

- **Nominated individual** — makes sure the responsible person's duties are met and resourced.
- **Registered manager** — arranges the fire risk assessment, keeps the fire safety log, sets safe night staffing, and arranges training and drills.
- **Shift leaders** — take charge when the alarm sounds and carry out daily checks.
- **All staff** — know the evacuation strategy and residents' PEEPs, and keep escape routes clear.

## 15. Training

All staff complete fire safety training at induction, before working a shift, covering the evacuation strategy, using evacuation equipment, and the fire procedure. It is refreshed every year, with drills in between. Shift leaders and fire marshals receive further training. Agency staff are shown the fire procedure at the start of their first shift. Training is recorded on the training matrix.

## 16. Records

The fire risk assessment, the fire safety log, PEEPs, drill records and training are kept by the registered manager. Fires, false alarms and near misses are recorded through the site's concerns process. All are kept for the periods set in Record Keeping and Retention.
