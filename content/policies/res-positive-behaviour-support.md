## 1. Purpose

Some residents sometimes behave in ways that can hurt them or others, or that limit their lives: shouting, hitting out, refusing care, walking with purpose and trying to leave, going into other people's rooms. That behaviour almost always has a reason — pain, fear, boredom, noise, a change in routine, not being understood. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} uses positive behaviour support to understand the reasons and improve the resident's quality of life, and so reduce the behaviour, rather than simply trying to control it.

## 2. Scope

It applies to every resident whose behaviour is described as challenging or distressed, including people living with dementia, people with a learning disability, autistic people and people with mental health needs. It applies to everyone who works in the home, on every shift, including agency staff.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 9: Person-centred care, and Regulation 13: Safeguarding service users from abuse and improper treatment — which forbids control or restraint that is not necessary or proportionate.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005, sections 5 and 6 — when acts done to protect a person who lacks capacity are lawful, and the limits on restraint.
- Human Rights Act 1998 — freedom from degrading treatment, the right to liberty and the right to respect for private life.
- Health and Care Act 2022 — registered providers must make sure staff receive training on learning disability and autism appropriate to their role, to the standards in the Oliver McGowan Code of Practice.
- NICE guideline NG97, Dementia: assessment, management and support; and NICE guideline NG11, Challenging behaviour and learning disabilities: prevention and interventions.
- Department of Health, Positive and Proactive Care: reducing the need for restrictive interventions (2014).
- Restraint Reduction Network Training Standards — the standard for any training that includes restrictive practices.
- STOMP — the NHS England programme to stop the over-medication of people with a learning disability, autism or both.

## 4. Our policy

- Behaviour is communication. Our first task is to understand what the resident is telling us.
- We improve the resident's life — what they do, who they see, how much control they have — because that is what reduces distress over time.
- We plan ahead so that staff on every shift know how to prevent distress, how to spot it building, and how to respond calmly.
- We do not punish residents, withdraw things they value as a consequence of behaviour, or use threats.
- We use restrictive practices only as a last resort, in line with our Restraint and Restrictive Practice policy.
- We keep other residents safe and treat harm between residents seriously.
- We look after the staff who support residents in distress.

## 5. The behaviour support plan

Every resident whose behaviour puts them or others at risk, or seriously limits their life, has a behaviour support plan as part of their care plan. It is written with the resident, their family and, where possible, a specialist such as the community mental health team for older people, the community learning disability team or a behaviour support practitioner. It is based on an assessment of why the behaviour happens, using what staff record by day and night. It sets out:

- the resident's life history, what they enjoy, and what a good day looks like for them here;
- what the behaviour looks like, and what it seems to achieve for the resident;
- what tends to trigger it — pain, hunger, noise in the lounge, a busy dining room, a particular resident, the shift change, being rushed, personal care;
- proactive strategies — what we do every day to keep the resident well and settled, such as familiar staff, a predictable routine, choice, meaningful activity, access to the garden, and good health care;
- early warning signs, and what to do when you see them — offering a walk, a drink, a quieter room, music, or a favourite activity;
- what to do if the behaviour happens — how to keep the resident, other residents and staff safe;
- what helps at night, when there are fewer staff and the resident may be disorientated;
- what to do afterwards to help the resident recover;
- any restrictive practice that has been agreed, why it is needed, and how it will be reduced.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 Getting to know the resident

Read the behaviour support plan, as well as the care plan, before you first support the resident, and check the notes and handover for recent changes or incidents. The shift leader allocates staff the resident knows where possible.

### 6.2 Day to day

Follow the proactive strategies. Keep to the routine the plan describes, explain what you are going to do, offer choices, and do not rush. Watch the environment: turn down a television nobody is watching, reduce clutter and noise, and make sure the resident can find their room and the toilet. Notice what the resident enjoys and what upsets them, and tell the key worker so the plan can be improved.

### 6.3 When you see early warning signs

Do what the plan says. In general: stay calm, lower your voice, give the resident space, reduce demands, and do not argue or correct them. Leave a task for later if it is causing distress and can wait safely. Do not crowd or corner the resident. A resident who wants to walk should be supported to walk safely, not made to sit down.

### 6.4 If the behaviour happens

- Keep everyone safe. Move other residents away calmly, or help them to another room, rather than moving the distressed resident if you can avoid it.
- Call for a colleague or the shift leader. Do not deal with a serious incident alone.
- Give the resident space. Do not use physical intervention unless it is in their plan and you have been trained in it, or as an absolute last resort to prevent serious harm.
- Phone 999 if anyone is in immediate danger.
- Never shut a resident in their room or any other space. That is seclusion, and it is not allowed.
- Once the resident is calm, follow the plan's recovery steps. Do not discuss the incident with them until they are ready.

### 6.5 When another resident is affected

If a resident hurts, frightens or behaves sexually towards another resident, look after the resident who was affected first: check them for injury, comfort them. Tell the shift leader straight away. The registered manager decides whether it must be referred to safeguarding or notified to CQC, and how both residents will be kept safe. A resident's distress does not stop harm to another resident being treated as a safeguarding concern.

### 6.6 Afterwards

Record what happened in the daily notes and through the site's concerns process as an incident: what happened before, during and after, the time and place, who was there, what you did and what helped. These records show patterns that help us understand the behaviour. The shift leader talks with you afterwards and checks that you are all right. If anyone was hurt, follow our Accidents, Incidents and Near Misses policy.

### 6.7 Health and medicines

A change in behaviour may be the first sign of pain, infection, constipation, dehydration, a side effect or delirium, especially in residents who cannot easily tell us. Tell the shift leader so that a health check can be arranged with the GP. Medicine used to manage behaviour, including antipsychotics, must be prescribed, recorded on the medicines record, given only as directed, and reviewed regularly with the prescriber. We support STOMP and ask for medicines reviews where a medicine may no longer be needed.

## 7. Reviewing the plan

A senior reviews the behaviour support plan with the resident and others involved at the interval the plan sets, after any serious incident, and when incidents become more or less frequent. The registered manager looks at incident records across the home to spot patterns by time, place and staffing, and makes sure restrictive practices are reducing.

## 8. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure behaviour support plans are in place, specialists are involved, staff are trained, incidents and restrictive practices are reviewed, and safeguarding referrals and notifications are made.
- **Seniors and key workers** — write and review plans with specialists, and tell staff about changes.
- **Shift leaders** — allocate staff the resident knows, brief agency staff on each plan, lead the response to incidents, and debrief staff.
- **Care staff** — follow the plan, record and report, and tell us what they learn about the resident.

## 9. Training

All care staff complete training in supporting people living with dementia and in positive behaviour support during induction, and training on learning disability and autism appropriate to their role. Staff who support a resident with a behaviour support plan are shown the plan before they work with them. Any training that includes physical intervention is certified against the Restraint Reduction Network Training Standards. Training is refreshed at the intervals on the training matrix.

## 10. Records

Behaviour support plans, the assessments and observation records behind them, incident records, debriefs and reviews are kept on the resident's record for as long as our retention schedule requires (see Record Keeping and Retention).
