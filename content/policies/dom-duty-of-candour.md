## 1. Purpose

When something goes wrong in a client's care, they and their family have a right to know what happened, to hear us say sorry, and to know what we will do about it. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} is open and honest with clients at all times, and how we meet the legal duty of candour when a client is seriously harmed.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works for us, including care workers visiting alone, office and on-call staff, and managers. It covers every client, and every incident that happens while we are providing their care — in their home, in the community or on the way to hospital.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 20: Duty of candour — a general duty to act in an open and transparent way with clients and the people acting for them, and a specific duty to follow set steps when a notifiable safety incident happens. Failing to notify the person, or to do so in the way the regulation requires, is a criminal offence.
- Compensation Act 2006, section 2 — an apology, or an offer of treatment or other redress, is not in itself an admission of negligence or breach of statutory duty.
- Care Quality Commission, guidance on Regulation 20: Duty of candour — how CQC expects the duty to be met.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — who can act for a client who lacks capacity.

## 4. Our policy

- We are open with clients about anything that goes wrong in their care, whether or not it caused harm, and whether or not it meets the threshold below. This is the general duty of openness.
- When a notifiable safety incident happens, we follow every step of the duty of candour, as soon as reasonably practicable.
- Saying sorry is the right thing to do. It is not an admission of liability, and nobody will be criticised for apologising.
- We support staff who report honestly. A care worker who reports a mistake is treated fairly; covering up is not tolerated.

## 5. What a notifiable safety incident is

For a provider like us, which is not a health service body, a notifiable safety incident is an unintended or unexpected incident that happened to a client while we were providing care, and that, in the reasonable opinion of a health care professional, either:

- appears to have resulted in the client's death, where the death relates directly to the incident rather than to the natural course of their illness or condition;
- appears to have resulted in an impairment of the client's sensory, motor or intellectual functions which has lasted, or is likely to last, for a continuous period of at least 28 days;
- appears to have resulted in changes to the structure of the client's body, such as a fracture;
- appears to have resulted in the client experiencing prolonged pain or prolonged psychological harm, meaning pain or harm they have experienced, or are likely to experience, for a continuous period of at least 28 days;
- appears to have resulted in the shortening of the client's life expectancy; or
- requires treatment by a health care professional to prevent the client's death, or to prevent an injury that, if untreated, would lead to one of the outcomes above.

The incident does not have to be anyone's fault. A fall during a transfer, a missed time-critical medicine, a pressure ulcer that developed because of our care, or a scald from bath water could each be a notifiable safety incident if the harm meets this threshold.

Care workers are not health care professionals. Unless the registered manager is a registered health professional, they ask the client's GP, the community nurse or the hospital for their view of the harm, and records whose opinion was given. If the opinion is not yet clear, we follow the duty: it is better to be open too early than too late.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 If something goes wrong on a visit

- Make the client safe, and get medical help if they need it. Call 999 in an emergency.
- Phone the office or on-call straight away. Do not leave until you have been told what to do, unless you are in danger.
- Be honest with the client about what has happened, and say you are sorry it happened. Leave the formal duty of candour conversation to the manager.
- Record the incident through the site's concerns process before the end of your shift, as Accidents, Incidents and Near Misses sets out.

### 6.2 Deciding whether the duty applies

{{registered_manager}} reviews every incident report within one working day, and decides whether it may be a notifiable safety incident. The decision, the reasons and the health care professional's opinion are recorded with the incident, even where the answer is no.

### 6.3 Telling the client, in person

As soon as reasonably practicable, the registered manager, or a senior manager they name, tells the relevant person in person that the incident has happened. The relevant person is the client or, where the client has died, is under 16 and not competent, or lacks capacity to deal with the matter, a person lawfully acting on their behalf, such as an attorney, a deputy or the next of kin. Our standard is to do this within two working days of deciding that the duty applies, at a time and place that suits them — usually the client's home, or with a family member who lives elsewhere.

The conversation:

- gives a true account of all the facts we know about the incident at that point;
- explains what further enquiries we will make;
- includes an apology — a sincere expression of sorrow or regret for what happened;
- offers reasonable support, such as help to understand what happened, an advocate or interpreter, a change of care worker, or contact details for other help.

It is recorded in writing and kept securely.

### 6.4 Writing to them

The in-person conversation is followed by a letter to the relevant person. Our standard is to send it within ten working days of the conversation. It sets out what was said: the facts as we know them, the enquiries we will make, the results of any enquiries already done, and an apology. When our enquiries are complete, we write again with the findings and what we are changing. We keep a copy of every letter.

### 6.5 If we cannot reach them

If the relevant person cannot be contacted, or does not wish to speak to us, we respect that, and we keep a written record of every attempt to contact them or speak to them. We tell them they can get in touch with us at any time.

## 7. How the duty fits with other processes

The duty runs alongside other processes, and does not replace them:

- the incident is investigated under Accidents, Incidents and Near Misses;
- abuse or neglect, including a neglectful missed visit, is referred to the local authority under Safeguarding;
- deaths and serious injuries are notified to CQC under Regulations 16 and 18 of the Care Quality Commission (Registration) Regulations 2009, as Reportable Incidents sets out. A CQC notification does not meet the duty of candour, and the duty of candour does not replace a notification;
- the client or family may also make a complaint, which is handled under Complaints.

Where the police or a safeguarding enquiry are involved, the registered manager agrees with them what can be shared, so that an investigation is not harmed. We still tell the relevant person that the incident happened and apologise.

## 8. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — decides whether the duty applies, holds or oversees each conversation, sends the letters, and makes sure records are complete.
- **Nominated individual** — makes sure the company meets the duty, and checks compliance through our governance checks.
- **Office and on-call staff** — give advice at the time, and make sure the registered manager knows the same day.
- **Care workers** — make clients safe, report every incident at once, and are honest with clients.

## 9. Training

All staff learn about openness and the duty of candour at induction, and refresh it every year with incident reporting. The registered manager, and anyone who holds duty of candour conversations, are trained in the regulation and in having difficult conversations.

## 10. Records

The decision on the duty, the health care professional's opinion, the record of the conversation, copies of every letter and records of attempts to make contact are kept with the incident through the site's concerns process, for the periods set in Record Keeping and Retention, and reviewed every month with all incidents.
