## 1. Purpose

Some clients who cannot make decisions about their care need such close support to keep them safe that, in law, they are deprived of their liberty — even in their own home, and even when everyone acts with the best intentions. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} recognises when that may be happening, and what we do so that it is only ever done with lawful authority.

## 2. Scope

It applies to every client aged 16 or over who receives care from us in their own home, including in supported living and extra care housing, and to everyone who works for us.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Human Rights Act 1998 and Article 5 of the European Convention on Human Rights — the right to liberty. Nobody may be deprived of their liberty except by a procedure the law sets out.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — a deprivation of liberty of someone who lacks capacity to consent to it is lawful only if authorised. In a person's own home this needs an order of the Court of Protection.
- The Supreme Court's judgment in P v Cheshire West and Chester Council (2014) — the "acid test" for when care amounts to a deprivation of liberty.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 13: Safeguarding service users from abuse and improper treatment — a person must not be deprived of their liberty for the purpose of care without lawful authority.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 Code of Practice and Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards Code of Practice.

The Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards (DoLS), under which care homes and hospitals ask the local authority for an authorisation, do not apply in a person's own home. The Liberty Protection Safeguards, which were meant to replace DoLS and cover all settings, have not been brought into force.

## 4. What a deprivation of liberty is

A person is deprived of their liberty when all of these apply:

- they are under continuous supervision and control;
- they are not free to leave — meaning to move away permanently, not just to go out for a walk;
- they lack the capacity to consent to those arrangements;
- the arrangements are the responsibility of the state — for example, the care is arranged or paid for by a local authority or the NHS, or a public body knows about it.

It does not matter that the person seems content, that they are not trying to leave, or that the arrangements are for their own good, or the same as anyone with their condition would need.

Signs to look out for at home include:

- the front door is locked and the client cannot open it or does not have a key;
- care workers or family would stop the client if they tried to leave, or would bring them back;
- the client is supervised all day and night, by us, family or other services together;
- door sensors, cameras or tracking devices are used to monitor them;
- equipment such as bed rails or a lap belt restricts their movement;
- the client is given medicine to calm them, or their medicine is hidden in food;
- family or care workers decide who the client may see and where they may go.

## 5. Our policy

- We support people in the least restrictive way possible, and look first for ways to keep them safe that do not restrict them.
- We do not deprive anyone of their liberty without lawful authority. We do not lock a client in, or agree to a family's request to do so, unless it is authorised.
- When a client's care may amount to a deprivation of liberty, we tell the body that funds or arranges their care in writing, and ask them to apply to the Court of Protection.
- We keep raising the matter until it is resolved, and we treat an unauthorised deprivation of liberty as a safeguarding concern.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 Care workers

If you notice any of the signs above, or you are asked to lock a door, take a key, stop a client going out, or use any equipment or medicine to restrict them, tell the office the same day. Do not stop doing what the care plan says until a senior has spoken to you, unless it is plainly unsafe — then phone on-call at once. Write down what you saw and what you were asked to do.

### 6.2 The registered manager

When a possible deprivation of liberty is reported, or is found at assessment or review, {{registered_manager}}:

- checks that the client's capacity to consent to their care arrangements has been assessed and recorded;
- reviews the care with the client, family and professionals involved, and looks for a less restrictive way of keeping them safe;
- if the restrictions are still needed and may amount to a deprivation of liberty, writes to the local authority, or the integrated care board if the NHS funds the care, setting out the restrictions and asking them to consider an application to the Court of Protection;
- records what was sent, to whom and when, and follows it up until there is an answer;
- updates the care plan so that care workers know what the restrictions are and that they must be kept to the minimum.

### 6.3 People who pay for their own care

The state can still be responsible where the client pays for their own care, especially where the local authority knows about the arrangements. If a self-funding client may be deprived of their liberty, the registered manager tells the local authority's adult social care team in the same way.

### 6.4 While an application is waiting

Court applications can take time. Meanwhile we keep restrictions to the minimum needed, review them regularly, record why each one is needed, and make sure the client's views and family's views are heard. If the restrictions increase or the client is objecting, we tell the commissioner again, as the case may need to be treated as more urgent.

### 6.5 When the Court makes an order

The registered manager keeps a copy of the order, makes sure the care plan follows it and any conditions in it, and tells the commissioner when the client's needs change so that the order can be reviewed. An order usually sets a date by which it must be reviewed; we remind the commissioner well before that date. The registered manager checks CQC's current guidance on notifications under Regulation 18 of the Care Quality Commission (Registration) Regulations 2009 and notifies CQC where it applies.

### 6.6 When nothing is done

If the commissioner does not respond, or a family insists on restrictions that have not been authorised, the registered manager raises it with the local authority's safeguarding team and records it through the site's concerns process. In an emergency, act to keep the client safe, using no more restriction than is necessary, and report it the same day.

## 7. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — identifies possible deprivations of liberty, asks the commissioner in writing to apply to the Court of Protection, follows up, and makes sure care plans follow any order.
- **Seniors** — look for restrictions at assessment and review, and record capacity assessments where trained to.
- **Office and on-call staff** — pass reports to the registered manager the same day.
- **Care workers** — recognise restrictions, report them, and never restrict a client beyond what the care plan says.

## 8. Training

All staff learn about the Mental Capacity Act, restrictions and deprivation of liberty in induction and refresh it every year. The registered manager and seniors who assess and review care complete further training on deprivation of liberty in community settings.

## 9. Records

Capacity assessments, best interests decisions, the restrictions in place and why, letters to commissioners and their replies, and any Court of Protection order are kept on the client's record for as long as we keep it (see Record Keeping and Retention).
