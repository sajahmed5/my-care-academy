## 1. Purpose

A pressure ulcer is damage to the skin and the tissue beneath it, caused by pressure, often combined with friction or moisture, usually over a bony part of the body such as the heels, hips or bottom. Most pressure ulcers can be prevented, and care staff are often the first to see the early signs. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} identifies people at risk, prevents skin damage, and acts quickly when it starts.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who gives or plans care, and to every person we support, in their own home or in a care home. It covers pressure ulcers, including those caused by medical devices, and moisture-associated skin damage.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment — risks to health are assessed and reduced, and we work with other professionals to provide safe care.
- Regulation 13: Safeguarding service users from abuse and improper treatment — neglect includes failing to provide care that prevents avoidable harm.
- NICE guideline CG179, Pressure ulcers: prevention and management, and NICE quality standard QS89, Pressure ulcers.
- NHS England and NHS Improvement, Pressure ulcers: revised definition and measurement — the categories used to describe pressure damage.
- Department of Health and Social Care, Safeguarding adults protocol: pressure ulcers and the interface with a safeguarding enquiry — how to decide whether a pressure ulcer needs a safeguarding referral.
- Care Quality Commission (Registration) Regulations 2009, Regulation 18 — notification of serious injuries and of abuse or allegations of abuse.

## 4. Our policy

- Every person's risk of pressure damage is assessed when we start supporting them and reviewed regularly.
- Everyone at risk has a skin care plan, followed on every visit or shift.
- Care staff check skin during personal care and report any change the same day.
- We work closely with district nurses, GPs, tissue viability nurses and equipment services, and follow their advice.
- Any pressure ulcer is recorded, reported and reviewed, so that we learn from it.

## 5. Who is at risk

People are at greater risk if they spend long periods in a bed or chair, cannot change position by themselves, are incontinent, are underweight or not eating and drinking well, have diabetes, poor circulation or reduced feeling, are very ill or at the end of life, have had a pressure ulcer before, or use devices such as catheters, oxygen tubing, splints or tight footwear that press on the skin.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 Assessing risk

When we start supporting someone, a trained senior or the shift leader assesses their risk using a recognised tool, such as the Waterlow or Braden scale or PURPOSE-T, together with their own judgement and a check of the person's skin, with their consent. The assessment is repeated when the care plan is reviewed, and whenever the person's health, mobility or continence changes. The result and the actions are recorded in the person's care plan.

### 6.2 The skin care plan

For each person at risk, the care plan sets out, under the familiar headings of skin, surface, keep moving, incontinence and moisture, and nutrition:

- which areas of skin to check, and how often;
- the pressure-relieving equipment to use — mattress, cushion, heel protection — and how to check it is working;
- how often the person should change position, how, and with what equipment;
- how to keep skin clean and dry, and which products to use;
- how to support eating and drinking;
- what the person and their family know about preventing pressure damage and what they have agreed.

### 6.3 Checking skin

Check the person's skin during personal care, with their consent, paying attention to heels, ankles, hips, the base of the spine, buttocks, elbows, shoulders, the back of the head and ears, and anywhere a device touches. On darker skin, redness may not show: look for skin that is darker, purplish or bluish than the surrounding area, and feel for heat, coolness, swelling or hardness. Ask the person if any area is painful.

### 6.4 If you find damage

If you see redness that does not fade within a short time of relieving pressure, discolouration, blistering, broken skin or a wound, or the person reports pain in a pressure area:

- relieve the pressure straight away, if you can safely;
- report it the same day — to the office or on-call for people we support at home, or to the shift leader in a care home;
- record what you saw, where, its size and appearance, and what you did. Do not try to treat or dress a wound yourself unless it is a task you have been trained and delegated to do.

The office or the shift leader then contacts the district nurse, who assesses and treats the wound and advises on care and equipment. If the district nurse cannot come promptly and the damage is getting worse, contact the GP.

### 6.5 Repositioning and equipment

Help people change position as their plan says, using the right equipment and technique so that skin is not dragged. Record each change of position on the repositioning record where the plan asks. Check that mattresses and cushions are in place, switched on and set correctly where they are powered, and not bottoming out. Report faulty equipment at once.

In a person's own home, equipment comes from the community equipment service through the district nurse or occupational therapist, and our visits may be the only times the person changes position. If the person needs more frequent repositioning than our visits allow, we tell the commissioner and the district nurse straight away. In a care home, repositioning continues through the day and night, and the shift leader makes sure night staff know who needs it.

### 6.6 Continence and moisture

Change continence products promptly, clean skin gently with the products in the plan, dry it well and apply barrier products as prescribed. Moisture damage looks different from pressure damage but is also reported and recorded.

### 6.7 Nutrition and hydration

Good nutrition and fluids help prevent skin damage and help it heal. Follow the person's eating and drinking plan, and report poor intake (see Nutrition and Hydration).

### 6.8 When a person declines

A person with capacity may decline repositioning, equipment or skin checks. Explain the risks, offer alternatives, record the conversation, and tell your manager so the district nurse can be involved and the plan reviewed. If the person lacks capacity to make the decision, it is made in their best interests.

## 7. Reporting and learning

Every pressure ulcer is recorded through the site's concerns process, whether it developed while in our care or was already present when we started. The registered manager:

- reviews how it happened and whether it could have been prevented, with the district nurse;
- uses the Department of Health and Social Care's safeguarding protocol to decide whether a safeguarding referral is needed, for example where care was not given as planned;
- decides whether it must be notified to CQC under Regulation 18, following CQC's current guidance;
- considers whether the duty of candour applies (see Duty of Candour);
- looks for patterns across everyone we support and makes changes.

## 8. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure risks are assessed and plans followed, reviews every pressure ulcer, and makes referrals and notifications.
- **Seniors and shift leaders** — assess risk, write skin care plans, contact district nurses, and check that repositioning and equipment checks happen.
- **Care workers and care staff** — check skin, reposition people, look after equipment, record and report.

## 9. Training

All staff complete pressure ulcer prevention and skin care training during induction, as part of the Care Certificate, and refresh it every year. Staff who assess risk are trained in the tool we use. Competence is checked through observation and spot checks and recorded on each person's file.

## 10. Records

Risk assessments, skin care plans, repositioning records, equipment checks, reports to district nurses and their advice, body maps and incident records are kept on the person's record, and incidents through the concerns process, for as long as our retention schedule requires.
