## 1. Purpose

Many people want to die at home, and good care at home makes that possible. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} supports clients at the end of their lives, respects the decisions they have made in advance, and what care workers do when a client dies — expectedly or not — so that the client, their family and our staff are treated with dignity at a very hard time.

## 2. Scope

It applies to all staff who care for or are in contact with clients who are approaching the end of life or who have died, and to the office and on-call staff who support them.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — advance decisions to refuse treatment, lasting powers of attorney for health and welfare, and best interests decisions.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 9: Person-centred care, Regulation 10: Dignity and respect, and Regulation 11: Need for consent.
- Care Quality Commission (Registration) Regulations 2009, Regulation 16 — notifying CQC of the death of a person using the service.
- Coroners and Justice Act 2009 — deaths that must be referred to the coroner, and the medical examiner system that reviews other deaths.
- NICE guideline NG31: Care of dying adults in the last days of life, and NICE guideline NG142: End of life care for adults: service delivery.
- Resuscitation Council UK guidance on decisions about cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and on the ReSPECT process.

## 4. Our policy

- We plan care around what the client wants: where they want to be cared for and to die, who they want with them, and what matters to them.
- We follow valid advance decisions and respect the client's wishes about treatment.
- We work closely with the GP, district nurses and any palliative care team, and tell them promptly when things change.
- Care workers never decide on their own that a client should not be resuscitated. That decision is made by a doctor or senior clinician.
- We support families and our own staff before and after a death.

## 5. Planning ahead

When a client's health is deteriorating, or they want to talk about the future, the registered manager or a senior offers them the chance to plan ahead, and with their consent involves their family and the GP. The care plan records:

- where the client would like to be cared for and to die, and their wishes for their care, comfort, faith and culture;
- whether they have a lasting power of attorney for health and welfare, and who the attorney is;
- whether they have an advance decision to refuse treatment, and where it is kept;
- whether there is a DNACPR decision or a ReSPECT form, and where the original is kept;
- who to contact, and when, including at night.

### 5.1 Advance decisions to refuse treatment

An advance decision lets a person aged 18 or over, while they have capacity, refuse specific medical treatment for a time when they lack capacity. If it refuses life-sustaining treatment it must be in writing, signed and witnessed, and say that it applies even if the person's life is at risk. A valid advance decision that applies to the situation must be followed. Keep a copy or note of it on the care record, and tell paramedics and doctors about it. If you are unsure whether it applies, tell the health professional and let them decide.

### 5.2 DNACPR and ReSPECT

A DNACPR (do not attempt cardiopulmonary resuscitation) decision is made by a doctor or senior clinician, with the person or, if they lack capacity, with those close to them. A ReSPECT form (Recommended Summary Plan for Emergency Care and Treatment) records a person's wishes and clinical recommendations for emergencies, including CPR. The original form stays in the client's home, where paramedics can find it. Its location is written in the care plan. A DNACPR decision applies only to CPR; the client still receives all other care and treatment.

## 6. Caring for a client at the end of life

- Follow the care plan, and keep the client clean, comfortable, and as pain-free as possible.
- Offer drinks and mouth care, and do not force food or fluids.
- Turn and reposition as planned, and watch the client's skin.
- Tell the office and the district nurse at once if the client seems to be in pain, distressed, breathless or agitated, or if their condition changes.
- Anticipatory ("just in case") medicines prescribed for symptoms at the end of life are given by the district nurse or other health professional, not by care workers, unless the task has been delegated to you and you have been trained.
- Support the family, and tell the office if they are struggling.

## 7. When a client dies

### 7.1 An expected death

If the client's death was expected, and the care plan says what to do, follow it — whether you were with them or arrive to find they have died. Usually this means:

- Stay calm. If family are present, offer them comfort and time.
- Do not call 999 unless the care plan says to, or you are unsure the death was expected.
- Phone the district nurse or GP surgery (or the out-of-hours service), who will arrange for the death to be verified. Care workers do not verify death.
- Phone the office or on-call.
- After the death has been verified, and if the family wish it, you may help to wash and lay out the client, following any religious or cultural wishes. Leave any medical devices in place unless the nurse says otherwise.
- The family contact a funeral director of their choice.

### 7.2 An unexpected death or finding a client who has died

- Call 999. Tell the call handler what you have found and follow their instructions, including about CPR. Start CPR unless there is a valid DNACPR decision or ReSPECT recommendation against it, or the call handler tells you otherwise.
- Do not move the client or anything around them, unless the ambulance service tells you to, and touch as little as possible.
- Phone the office or on-call, who will contact the family.
- Stay until the ambulance or police arrive, unless you are unsafe.

An unexpected death is reported to the coroner, and the police usually attend on the coroner's behalf. This is routine. Answer their questions honestly; the office will support you.

### 7.3 After any death

- Write a full account in the daily notes as soon as you can: what you found, the time, who you contacted, and what was done.
- Do not remove anything from the home. The client's medicines stay in the home for the family to return to the pharmacy, unless a nurse or the police ask otherwise.
- Make sure the home is secure and keys are dealt with as our Key Holding and Key Safes policy sets out.
- Cancel the client's remaining visits, and tell any care worker who was due to visit.

## 8. What the office and registered manager do

- Tell the commissioner, if the care is funded, as the contract requires.
- Notify the Care Quality Commission without delay where Regulation 16 requires it — where the death happened while we were providing care, or may have been caused by our care or the way it was provided.
- If there is any concern that neglect, abuse or our care contributed to the death, raise a safeguarding concern with the local authority and record it through the site's concerns process.
- Keep the care records secure; the coroner or medical examiner may ask for them.
- Contact the family to offer condolences and information about bereavement support.
- Check on the care workers involved, offer a debrief, and give them time off or support if they need it.

## 9. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure end of life wishes and advance decisions are recorded, works with health professionals, notifies CQC and commissioners, and supports staff and families.
- **Office and on-call staff** — support care workers when a client dies, contact the family, and cancel visits.
- **Care workers** — give compassionate end of life care, follow the care plan, know where advance decisions and DNACPR or ReSPECT forms are kept, and follow this policy when a client dies.

## 10. Training

All care workers complete end of life care and basic life support training during induction, and refresh them every year.

## 11. Records

End of life wishes, advance decisions, DNACPR and ReSPECT information, and the account of a death are kept on the client's care record for as long as our record retention schedule requires (see Record Keeping and Retention). CQC notifications and any safeguarding concern are kept through the concerns process.
