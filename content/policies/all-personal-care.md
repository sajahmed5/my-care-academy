## 1. Purpose

Help with washing, dressing, using the toilet and cleaning teeth is the most intimate care we give, and the care people notice most. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} gives personal care, continence care and mouth care safely, in the way each person wants, protecting their dignity and their health.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who gives or plans personal care, and to every person we support, in their own home or in a care home.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 9: Person-centred care, Regulation 10: Dignity and respect, Regulation 11: Need for consent, and Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — consent to personal care, and best interests where a person cannot consent.
- NICE guideline NG48, Oral health for adults in care homes, and NICE quality standard QS151, Oral health in care homes — mouth care assessment, daily mouth care and access to dental treatment. We follow the same approach for people we support in their own homes.
- NICE guideline NG21, Home care: delivering personal care and practical support to older people living in their own homes.
- NICE guidance on healthcare-associated infections in community care, including the care of long-term urinary catheters.
- Health and Safety Executive guidance on managing the risks from hot water and surfaces in health and social care.

## 4. Our policy

- Personal care is given the way the person wants it, at times that suit them, as their care plan records.
- We ask before every step, and respect a refusal.
- People do as much for themselves as they can.
- Privacy and dignity are protected throughout (see Dignity, Privacy and Respect).
- We use personal care as a chance to notice changes in a person's skin, mouth, mood and health, and we report them.
- Continence is promoted, not just managed. Pads are never a substitute for helping someone to the toilet.
- Everyone has a mouth care assessment and daily mouth care, and help to see a dentist.

## 5. What to do

### 5.1 Before you start

- Read the care plan: how the person likes to be washed and dressed, what they do for themselves, the equipment to use, and any preference about the gender of who helps.
- Wash your hands and put on the personal protective equipment the task needs.
- Close doors and curtains. Get everything ready so the person is not left exposed while you fetch things.
- Check the water temperature with your hand and, where provided, a thermometer, before anyone gets into a bath or shower. In a care home, bath and shower temperatures are controlled and checked as our Legionella and Water Safety policy sets out.

### 5.2 Washing and dressing

Explain what you are going to do and ask the person how they would like it done today. Use their own toiletries, towels and clothes. Keep them covered, wash from clean to dirty areas, and dry skin thoroughly, especially in folds and between toes. Apply creams as prescribed and recorded. Help them dress in clothes they have chosen, and with their hair, make-up, shaving and jewellery as they like. Shave with the person's own razor; never share razors.

Do not cut the toenails of anyone with diabetes, poor circulation or thickened nails: a podiatrist does this. Report any foot problems.

### 5.3 Noticing and reporting

While you help, look for red or broken skin, bruises, swelling, rashes, sores in the mouth, unusual smells, pain, and changes in how much the person can do. Report anything new to the office, on-call or the shift leader the same day, and record it. An unexplained bruise or injury may need a safeguarding report.

### 5.4 Refusals

If a person does not want personal care, respect it. Try again later, or offer a different way, a different time, or a different care worker. Record what you offered and what they said. Tell your manager if refusals continue, so the plan can be reviewed and any risk — to skin, health or dignity — managed with the person.

### 5.5 Continence

- Help people to use the toilet, commode or urinal when they need it and at the times their plan sets, and give them privacy.
- Respond quickly to requests for help and to signs that someone needs the toilet.
- Use continence products only as assessed, in the right size and type. Change them promptly, clean and dry the skin, and apply barrier cream if prescribed.
- Ask for a referral to the NHS continence service if a person's continence changes, or products do not seem right.
- Record bowel movements where the plan asks, and report constipation, diarrhoea, blood, or a change in urine such as a strong smell, darker colour or pain. Do not test urine with a dipstick unless a clinician has asked you to.

### 5.6 Catheter care

Care for a urinary catheter only as you have been trained and as the care plan, written with the district nurse, says. Wash your hands before and after. Keep the drainage bag below the level of the bladder and off the floor, avoid kinks, and keep the system closed. Empty and change bags as instructed. Report reduced drainage, leaking, pain, fever, confusion or blood straight away: these can be signs of a blockage or infection that needs prompt attention.

### 5.7 Mouth care

Each person's mouth care needs are assessed when we start supporting them and recorded in their care plan, including whether they have their own teeth, dentures or both, and who their dentist is. Then:

- help them clean their natural teeth twice a day, using their own soft toothbrush and a fluoride toothpaste, as the plan says;
- clean dentures daily, over a bowl of water or a towel so they do not break if dropped, and store them as the person prefers, usually out of the mouth overnight in a labelled pot;
- check the mouth for sores, bleeding, ulcers, loose or broken teeth, and dentures that no longer fit;
- report pain, difficulty eating, or changes in the mouth, and help the person arrange a dental appointment or a home visit from a dentist;
- in a care home, mark dentures with the resident's name where they agree, so they are not lost or mixed up.

People who cannot eat by mouth, are very unwell or are at the end of their life need more frequent mouth care, as their plan sets out.

## 6. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure personal care is planned and given well, that equipment and supplies are available, and that mouth care assessments are done for everyone.
- **Seniors and shift leaders** — assess and plan personal, continence and mouth care, arrange referrals, and check practice through observation and spot checks.
- **Care workers and care staff** — give care as planned, respect choices and privacy, and report changes.

## 7. Training

All staff learn personal care, continence care, infection prevention and dignity during induction, as part of the Care Certificate, and are observed before they give care alone. Mouth care training is given at induction and refreshed every year. Catheter care is taught and competence assessed before staff do it, with refreshers at the interval on the training matrix. Competence is recorded on each person's file.

## 8. Records

Care plans record each person's personal, continence and mouth care needs and preferences. Daily records note the care given, refusals, and changes noticed. Mouth care assessments, continence assessments and catheter care plans are kept on the person's record and reviewed with the care plan.
