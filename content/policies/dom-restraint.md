## 1. Purpose

Restraint is not only holding someone down. Locking a door, raising a bed rail, moving a walking frame out of reach, giving medicine to calm someone, or telling them they cannot go out are all ways of restricting a person. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} keeps restrictions on clients to the minimum, uses them only when the law allows, and makes sure each one is planned, recorded and reviewed.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works for us and to every client, in their own home, supported living or extra care housing. It covers restrictions we put in place, and restrictions put in place by family or others that we see or are asked to take part in.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Mental Capacity Act 2005, section 6 — restraint of a person who lacks capacity is lawful only if the person using it reasonably believes it is necessary to prevent harm to that person, and it is a proportionate response to the likelihood and seriousness of the harm. Restraint that goes further may be a deprivation of liberty and needs authorisation.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 13: Safeguarding service users from abuse and improper treatment — control or restraint that is not necessary or proportionate is treated as abuse.
- Human Rights Act 1998 — freedom from inhuman or degrading treatment (Article 3), the right to liberty (Article 5) and the right to respect for private life and home (Article 8).
- Department of Health, Positive and Proactive Care: reducing the need for restrictive interventions (2014).
- Restraint Reduction Network Training Standards.
- Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency guidance on the safe use of bed rails.
- NICE guideline NG11, Challenging behaviour and learning disabilities: prevention and interventions.

## 4. What counts as restraint

- Physical — holding or guiding a person against their will, or blocking their way.
- Mechanical — equipment that limits movement: bed rails, lap belts, harnesses, tables fixed across a chair, or a chair the person cannot get out of.
- Chemical — medicine given mainly to control behaviour, including "when required" sedatives.
- Environmental — locking doors or rooms, removing keys, walking aids, phones or other things the person needs, or setting a key safe so the person cannot get out.
- Surveillance — door sensors, pressure mats, cameras or trackers.
- Psychological — telling a person they are not allowed to do something, threats, or withholding something they want until they comply.

Some of these are part of good care when agreed with a person who has capacity — for example, a client who asks for bed rails to stop them rolling out of bed. They become restraint when the person does not agree or cannot.

## 5. Our policy

- We prevent the need for restraint by understanding people and supporting them well (see Positive Behaviour Support).
- Any restriction must be necessary to prevent harm, proportionate to that harm, the least restrictive option, and for the shortest time.
- Every planned restriction is assessed, agreed through a capacity assessment and best interests decision where the client cannot consent, written in the care plan, and reviewed.
- Care workers do not use planned physical restraint unless it is in the client's plan, agreed with the commissioner and the professionals involved, and they have been trained in the specific technique to the Restraint Reduction Network Training Standards.
- We never use restraint as punishment, for convenience, because there are not enough staff, or because a family asks us to without proper assessment.
- Restraint that may amount to a deprivation of liberty is referred so it can be authorised.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 Planned restrictions

When a restriction is being considered, a senior or the registered manager:

- checks first whether there is a less restrictive way of meeting the need;
- assesses the client's capacity to agree to it, and if they lack capacity, makes a best interests decision with family and professionals;
- for bed rails and other equipment, makes sure an assessment has been done by a suitably trained person, that the equipment is right for this client and this bed, and that it is checked for gaps and entrapment risks;
- writes it into the care plan: what it is, why it is needed, when it is used, how care workers use it, and when it will be reviewed;
- considers whether, with everything else in the client's care, it may amount to a deprivation of liberty (see Deprivation of Liberty).

### 6.2 Using an agreed restriction

Use the restriction exactly as the care plan says and no more. Check equipment before each use. Explain what you are doing to the client every time. Record its use in the daily notes where the plan asks you to. If the client is objecting or distressed, or it no longer seems right, stop if it is safe to, and tell the office the same day.

### 6.3 In an emergency

If you need to act to prevent serious and immediate harm — pulling a client back from a road, stopping them touching a hot cooker — use the least force for the shortest time, then let go. Afterwards:

- check the client for injury, and get medical help if needed;
- tell on-call straight away;
- record what happened, why you acted, what you did and for how long, through the site's concerns process as an incident, the same day.

### 6.4 Restrictions by family or others

If you see that a client is being restricted by family or anyone else — locked in, tied, sedated, kept from going out or seeing people — or you are asked to do it, do not take part. Tell the office the same day. The registered manager talks with the family, looks at whether the restriction is needed and lawful, and makes a safeguarding referral where the client may be at risk of abuse.

### 6.5 Medicines

Medicine used to calm a client is given only as prescribed, with a clear plan for when a "when required" dose may be given, and only after the plan's other steps have been tried. Each dose is recorded with the reason. Hiding medicine in food or drink without the client's knowledge is covert medication and is done only as our Covert Medication policy allows.

## 7. After any restriction is used

The senior or on-call manager talks with the care worker after any emergency restraint, and with the client when they are ready, about what happened and what could prevent it next time. The registered manager reviews every incident, decides whether it must be referred to safeguarding or notified to CQC, and updates the care plan.

## 8. Reviewing and reducing

Each planned restriction is reviewed at the date set in the care plan, after any incident, and when the client's needs change. The registered manager reviews restrictive practice across all clients regularly, looking for any that can be reduced or stopped, and reports the findings through our governance meetings.

## 9. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure restrictions are lawful, assessed, recorded and reviewed; reviews incidents; makes referrals and notifications; and leads on reducing restrictive practice.
- **Seniors** — assess and plan restrictions, write them into care plans, and debrief staff.
- **Office and on-call staff** — support care workers during and after incidents and pass reports to the registered manager.
- **Care workers** — use only the restrictions in the care plan, report and record every use outside it, and report restrictions by others.

## 10. Training

All care workers learn about restraint, restrictive practice and the Mental Capacity Act during induction and refresh it every year. Care workers who use equipment such as bed rails are trained to check and use it. Any training in physical intervention is certified against the Restraint Reduction Network Training Standards and given only to care workers who support a client whose plan includes it. Training is recorded on the training matrix.

## 11. Records

Assessments, best interests decisions, care plan entries for each restriction, records of use, incident reports and reviews are kept on the client's record, and incidents through the concerns process, for as long as our retention schedule requires.
