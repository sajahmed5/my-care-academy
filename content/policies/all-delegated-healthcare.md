## 1. Purpose

More and more people are supported at home and in care homes with health needs that were once met only by nurses. A registered health professional can delegate some of these tasks to care staff — giving insulin, feeding through a PEG tube, caring for a catheter or stoma — so that people get the care they need from the people who know them. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} decides whether to accept a delegated task, and how we make sure staff are trained, competent and supported to do it safely.

## 2. Scope

It applies to every healthcare task delegated to our staff by a registered health professional, for a named person we support. It applies to the registered manager, who agrees to delegations, and to every care worker or member of care staff who carries them out.

It does not cover the everyday support with medicines described in our Medicines Management policy, such as giving tablets from a pharmacy pack, which does not need a delegation.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment — staff have the qualifications, competence, skills and experience to provide care safely; Regulation 18: Staffing — staff receive the training they need; Regulation 11: Need for consent.
- Skills for Care and partners, guiding principles for delegated healthcare activities — how delegation should work between health and social care: person-centred, agreed by everyone involved, with training and competency for each person and each task, and clear accountability.
- The Nursing and Midwifery Council's Code, and the standards of the Health and Care Professions Council — the delegating professional's duty to delegate only to someone competent, and to give the support and supervision needed.
- NICE guideline NG67, Managing medicines for adults receiving social care in the community, and NICE guideline SC1, Managing medicines in care homes — where the delegated task involves medicines.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — the person's consent to the task, or a best interests decision where they lack capacity.

## 4. Our policy

- A delegated task is for one named person and one task at a time. Being competent to give insulin to one person does not make a care worker competent to give it to another.
- We accept a delegation only when we are satisfied it is safe: the task is suitable for care staff, we have staff who can be trained, the training and written instructions are provided, and our insurance covers it.
- No one carries out a delegated task until they have been trained and signed off as competent for that person and that task.
- Staff may decline a task they do not feel competent to do, and are never pressed to do it.
- Tasks that need clinical judgement, assessment or diagnosis stay with the health professional.

## 5. Typical delegated tasks

These are examples of tasks that may be delegated, depending on the person and on local arrangements:

- giving insulin by pen and checking blood glucose;
- feeding, giving fluids and giving medicines through a PEG or other feeding tube, and caring for the site;
- catheter care, changing catheter bags and recording output (but not inserting a catheter);
- stoma care and changing appliances;
- giving rescue medicines for epilepsy, such as buccal medicine;
- applying and removing compression hosiery, and simple dressings;
- oxygen therapy, nebulisers and suction of the mouth.

## 6. How a delegation is agreed

### 6.1 The request

The request comes from a registered health professional — usually a community or specialist nurse, but sometimes a GP, dietitian, physiotherapist or other professional. It is passed to {{registered_manager}}, who does not agree to it on the phone or on the spot.

### 6.2 Deciding whether to accept

The registered manager considers, with the professional:

- whether the task is stable and predictable for this person, so that it does not need clinical judgement each time;
- what could go wrong, and what staff should do if it does;
- whether the person, or their representative where they lack capacity, agrees to care staff doing it;
- which staff will do it, and whether enough of them can be trained to cover holidays, sickness and every visit or shift when it is needed;
- whether our insurance covers this task;
- whether funding for the extra time and training has been agreed, where that applies.

If the registered manager is not satisfied, they decline, explain why, and work with the professional on another arrangement. The task stays with the health professional until a delegation is agreed.

### 6.3 What the professional provides

Before staff start, the delegating professional provides:

- a written, person-specific plan or protocol for the task, including what to do if something goes wrong and when to call for help;
- training for the named staff, in person and with the person where possible;
- an assessment of each named member of staff's competence, signed off by the professional;
- a named contact for advice, and arrangements for review.

## 7. Training and competency

Each care worker or member of care staff is trained for the task and the person, and observed doing it until the professional is satisfied they are competent. The competency is signed off by the professional and recorded on the staff member's file, naming the person and the task.

Competency is reassessed:

- at the interval the professional sets, and at least every year;
- whenever the person's needs, equipment or plan change;
- when the staff member has not done the task for some time, or has made an error;
- when the staff member asks for a refresher.

The rota or allocation makes sure that only staff signed off for the task are sent to do it. If no competent member of staff is available, the office or shift leader tells the professional in good time so that the task is still done.

## 8. Carrying out a delegated task

- Follow the written plan exactly, and use only the equipment provided.
- Check the person's consent each time.
- Record what you did, the readings you took and anything unusual, as the plan says.
- If anything is outside what the plan describes — a reading out of range, a blocked tube, pain, bleeding, signs of infection — follow the plan's instructions, contact the professional or call 111 or 999 as the plan says, and tell the office or the shift leader.
- Do not go beyond the task you have been trained for, even if asked to by the person, their family or another professional.

Errors and near misses are reported straight away and recorded through the site's concerns process, as set out in Accidents, Incidents and Near Misses.

## 9. Accountability

Everyone involved stays accountable for their part:

- the delegating professional for deciding that the task can be delegated, for the training and competency assessment, and for continuing support and review;
- {{organisation}} for agreeing to the delegation, for making sure only competent staff do it, and for supervising our staff;
- the care worker or member of care staff for doing the task as they were trained, and for saying when they are not able to.

## 10. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — decides whether to accept each delegation, checks insurance, keeps a list of delegated tasks and the staff signed off for each, and arranges reviews.
- **Office staff and shift leaders** — make sure only signed-off staff are allocated to delegated tasks, and raise gaps early.
- **Care workers and care staff** — complete the training, carry out tasks only when signed off, record them, and report problems.

## 11. Training

Alongside person-specific training, staff complete general training relevant to the tasks they may do — such as diabetes awareness, or catheter and continence care — and refresh it as their training plan sets out. Training is recorded on the training matrix.

## 12. Records

The delegation agreement, the written plan, the professional's contact details and reviews are kept on the person's care record. Competency sign-offs are recorded on each staff member's file. Records of each task carried out are kept with the person's daily records. All are kept for the periods set in Record Keeping and Retention.
