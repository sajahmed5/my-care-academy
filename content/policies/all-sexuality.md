## 1. Purpose

Relationships, intimacy and sexuality matter to people throughout their lives, whatever their age, health or disability. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} respects the right of the people we support to have relationships and to express their sexuality and gender identity, while protecting them from abuse and exploitation, and makes clear the boundaries every member of staff must keep.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works for us, and to the people we support wherever they live. It covers friendships, partnerships and marriages, sexual relationships, sexual expression, sexual orientation and gender identity.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Human Rights Act 1998 — Article 8, the right to respect for private and family life, which includes relationships and sexual life.
- Equality Act 2010 — sexual orientation, gender reassignment, and marriage and civil partnership are protected characteristics; we must not discriminate.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — capacity to consent to sexual relations is decided by the person alone; section 27 means nobody, including a court or attorney, can consent to sex or marriage on another person's behalf.
- Sexual Offences Act 2003 — sexual activity without consent is a crime, and sexual activity between a care worker and a person with a mental disorder they care for is a crime whether or not the person appears to agree.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 10: Dignity and respect, and Regulation 13: Safeguarding service users from abuse and improper treatment.
- Care Quality Commission guidance on relationships and sexuality in adult social care services.

## 4. Our policy

- We treat the people we support as adults with the same rights as anyone else to relationships, privacy and a sexual life.
- We do not judge or discriminate because of a person's sexual orientation, gender identity, relationships or beliefs, and we challenge staff and others who do.
- We assume a person has capacity to make decisions about relationships and sex unless there is reason to doubt it, and we support them to decide.
- We protect people from sexual abuse and exploitation and act on every concern.
- No member of staff ever has a sexual or romantic relationship with a person we support.

## 5. Supporting people

### 5.1 Talking about relationships

Ask people, at assessment and at reviews, whether there is anything about their relationships, sexuality or gender identity they want us to know or to support. Some people will want to talk, and many will not; do not press. Record only what the person agrees to and what is needed to support them. A person's sexual orientation or gender identity is confidential and is shared only with their agreement.

### 5.2 Privacy

- Knock and wait before entering a person's room or home, and respect a closed door.
- Give people private time with their partner or visitor when they want it.
- If you walk in on a person who is masturbating or being intimate with someone, apologise, leave, and come back later. Do not comment or tell others.
- In a care home, couples can share a room if they both wish and it can be arranged, and residents can have visitors in their own room. See our Visiting policy.

### 5.3 Gender identity

Use the name and pronouns a person asks you to use. Respect how they wish to dress and present themselves, and their preferences about the sex of the care worker who gives intimate care. The protection the Equality Act 2010 gives for gender reassignment does not depend on a Gender Recognition Certificate or on any medical treatment.

### 5.4 Practical support

Support may include helping a person to meet friends or partners, to use a phone or the internet safely, to get information about sexual health and contraception, or to see their GP or a sexual health clinic. Staff do not arrange or pay for sexual services for anyone. If a person asks for support you are unsure about, tell the registered manager, who will take advice and agree a plan with the person.

## 6. Capacity and consent

Capacity to consent to sexual relations is about whether the person understands, in general terms, what sex involves, that they can say no, that the other person must also consent and keep consenting, and the risks to health, including pregnancy and infection. It is judged about sex in general, not about a particular partner. Capacity to consent to sex, to marriage, and to contact with a particular person are separate decisions.

If you think a person may not be able to consent to a sexual relationship, or that their partner may not be able to, tell the registered manager straight away. The registered manager decides whether a formal capacity assessment is needed, and takes advice from the local authority. Because no one can consent on another's behalf, if a person lacks capacity to consent to sex, sexual activity with them must be prevented, and the matter is raised as a safeguarding concern.

Where one partner in a long-standing relationship develops dementia, the registered manager works with both partners, their family and the local authority to support the relationship while protecting both of them. The couple's history matters, and this is handled with great care.

## 7. Safeguarding and sexual safety

Sexual abuse includes any sexual act a person has not consented to or could not consent to, indecent exposure, sexual harassment, being made to watch or take part in sexual images, and sexual exploitation. It can be committed by partners, family, visitors, other people we support, strangers, or staff.

If you see, hear or suspect sexual abuse:

- If someone is in immediate danger, call 999.
- Make the person safe and stay with them if you can.
- Do not wash the person, their clothes or bedding, or clean the area, unless you must for their health, as evidence may be lost.
- Tell the registered manager or on-call straight away, and record what you saw and heard, in the person's own words where you can.

The registered manager raises a safeguarding concern with the local authority, informs the police where a crime may have been committed, and notifies the Care Quality Commission as Regulation 18 of the Care Quality Commission (Registration) Regulations 2009 requires. The concern is recorded through the site's concerns process. Sexual behaviour between people we support that seems unwanted by one of them, or that causes distress to others, is also reported and assessed.

## 8. Staff boundaries

- A sexual or romantic relationship with a person we support, or with a person we supported in the past where this could be an abuse of the trust our role gave, is gross misconduct.
- Do not make sexual comments or jokes, or flirt with the people we support.
- Do not share your own sexual life or views on other people's relationships.
- If a person we support makes sexual advances or comments to you, stay calm, make the boundary clear kindly, and tell your manager. Staff are protected from sexual harassment too.

## 9. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — leads on this policy, decides when capacity should be assessed, agrees support plans for relationships, and acts on safeguarding concerns.
- **Seniors and shift leaders** — make sure care plans record what people want, and pass on concerns at once.
- **All staff** — respect people's privacy, relationships and identity, keep professional boundaries, and report concerns about abuse.

## 10. Training

All staff learn about dignity, equality, consent, professional boundaries and safeguarding, including sexual abuse, during induction and refresh it every year. Managers and seniors receive further training on capacity and consent to sexual relations.

## 11. Records

Information about a person's relationships, sexuality or gender identity is recorded only with their agreement or where needed to keep them safe, and kept confidentially on their care record. Capacity assessments and safeguarding concerns are kept on the care record and through the concerns process.
