## 1. Purpose

This policy sets out how {{organisation}} supports people to make their own decisions, how we assess whether someone has the capacity to make a particular decision, and how we act in their best interests when they do not. It makes sure we follow the Mental Capacity Act 2005 in everyday care, not only in formal assessments.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works for us, and to every decision about the care and support of a person aged 16 or over — from what they eat and wear to where they live and what medicines they take.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — the five principles, the test for capacity, best interests decisions, and protection for people who act in someone's best interests.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 Code of Practice — the guidance we must have regard to when we act for someone who lacks capacity.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 11: Need for consent — care is given only with consent, or in line with the Mental Capacity Act.
- Care Act 2014 — the duty on the local authority to provide an independent advocate for some people.
- Human Rights Act 1998 — the right to respect for private and family life, and to liberty.

## 4. The five principles

Everything we do follows the five principles of the Act:

- Assume capacity. We start from the assumption that a person can make their own decision unless it is shown that they cannot.
- Help people decide. We take every practical step to help someone make their own decision before we conclude that they cannot.
- Respect unwise decisions. A decision that seems unwise to us is not, on its own, a sign that someone lacks capacity.
- Best interests. Anything we do for someone who lacks capacity must be in their best interests.
- Least restrictive option. We choose the way that interferes least with the person's rights and freedom.

## 5. What to do

### 5.1 Everyday decisions

Most decisions are small and are made many times a day: getting up, washing, dressing, eating. Offer choices in a way the person understands — show two jumpers rather than asking an open question, speak slowly, use their language, pictures or signs, and choose the time of day when they are most alert. Record what helps them decide in their care plan. If a person refuses care, respect it, try again later or in a different way, and tell the office or the person in charge if a refusal could harm them.

### 5.2 When capacity is in doubt

Capacity is about one decision at one time. A person may be able to choose what to eat but not to manage their money, or be able to decide in the morning but not in the evening. Never say someone "lacks capacity" in general.

If you think a person may not be able to make a particular decision, tell {{registered_manager}} or the senior on duty. Do not carry out a formal assessment yourself unless you have been trained and asked to.

### 5.3 Assessing capacity

The person responsible for the decision assesses capacity — for care decisions this is usually the registered manager or a trained senior; for medical treatment it is the health professional proposing it. The assessment asks two questions:

- Is there an impairment of, or a disturbance in the functioning of, the person's mind or brain? This can be temporary, such as an infection or the effect of medication.
- Because of it, is the person unable to make this decision now? They are unable if they cannot understand the information relevant to the decision, retain it long enough to decide, use or weigh it, or communicate their decision in any way.

The assessment is recorded on the person's care record: the decision, the information given, how it was given, what the person said and did, and the conclusion with reasons.

### 5.4 Best interests decisions

When a person lacks capacity for a decision, whoever is making it must decide in their best interests. They:

- consider whether the person is likely to regain capacity and whether the decision can wait;
- involve the person as fully as possible;
- find out the person's past and present wishes, feelings, beliefs and values, including anything written down;
- consult family, friends, carers and anyone the person has named, and any attorney or deputy;
- do not decide on the basis of age, appearance, condition or behaviour;
- choose the least restrictive option.

Larger decisions — a change of where someone lives, serious medical treatment, covert medication — are made at a best interests meeting with the people who know the person, and recorded in full.

### 5.5 Lasting powers of attorney, deputies and advance decisions

Ask at assessment whether the person has a lasting power of attorney for health and welfare or for property and financial affairs, a deputy appointed by the Court of Protection, or an advance decision to refuse treatment. Check the document or the Office of the Public Guardian register, keep a copy or a note of it on file, and record what it covers. An attorney or deputy may only make the decisions their authority covers, and must also act in the person's best interests. A valid advance decision to refuse treatment must be followed.

### 5.6 Advocacy

A person who has difficulty taking part in decisions, and has nobody appropriate to support them, may be entitled to an independent advocate: an Independent Mental Capacity Advocate for serious medical treatment or a long-term move, or a Care Act advocate during assessments and reviews. Tell {{registered_manager}}, who will ask the local authority or the NHS body responsible. Anyone may also use a general advocacy service, and we will help them find one.

### 5.7 Restrictions and deprivation of liberty

If a person's care involves restricting them — locked doors, constant supervision, preventing them from leaving — tell {{registered_manager}} at once. It may amount to a deprivation of liberty, which must be authorised. See our Deprivation of Liberty policy.

## 6. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure capacity is assessed and recorded where it is in doubt, arranges best interests meetings and advocacy, and checks that care plans reflect the person's own decisions.
- **Senior staff** — carry out and record capacity assessments for care decisions when trained to, and check that daily records show choices being offered.
- **All staff** — assume capacity, support people to decide, respect their decisions, and report concerns about capacity or restrictions.

## 7. Training

All staff complete Mental Capacity Act training during induction, as part of the Care Certificate, and refresh it every year. Staff who assess capacity or lead best interests meetings complete further training first.

## 8. Records

Capacity assessments, best interests decisions, copies or notes of lasting powers of attorney and deputyships, and advance decisions are kept on the person's care record for as long as we keep their record (see Record Keeping and Retention).
