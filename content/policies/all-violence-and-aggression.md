## 1. Purpose

No one should be abused, threatened or assaulted at work, and no one should accept it as "part of the job". This policy sets out how {{organisation}} protects staff from violence and aggression — from the people we support, their families and visitors, and members of the public — how staff should respond when it happens, and how we support them afterwards.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works for us, wherever they work. It covers physical assault, threats, verbal abuse, racist, sexist or other discriminatory abuse, sexual harassment, and aggressive behaviour by phone, message or online.

Abuse or harassment from a colleague is dealt with under our Bullying and Harassment policy.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999 — the duty to assess and control the risk of violence to staff. The Health and Safety Executive defines work-related violence as any incident in which a person is abused, threatened or assaulted in circumstances relating to their work.
- Equality Act 2010, as amended by the Worker Protection (Amendment of Equality Act 2010) Act 2023 — employers must take reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment of their employees, including harassment by people who are not employees, such as the people they support and their families.
- Reporting of Injuries, Diseases and Dangerous Occurrences Regulations 2013 — some injuries to staff from violence at work must be reported to the Health and Safety Executive.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment, and Regulation 13: Safeguarding service users from abuse and improper treatment — which limits how staff may respond.

## 4. Our policy

- Your safety comes first. You may leave a situation where you feel at risk, and you will not be criticised for doing so.
- Every incident is reported, however minor, so that we can act on it and protect the next person.
- We distinguish between behaviour that is a sign of distress, illness or unmet need, and behaviour that is deliberate. Both are taken seriously; they are managed differently.
- We support staff who have been affected, and back them in reporting crimes to the police.
- Racist, sexist, homophobic and other discriminatory abuse, and sexual harassment, are never tolerated.

## 5. Preventing violence and aggression

### 5.1 Assessing the risk

When we assess a person before their care starts, and at every review, we ask about any history of aggressive behaviour, what triggers it, and what helps. In home care, the environmental risk assessment also covers others who live in or visit the home. Where there is a risk, it is written into the care plan with what staff should do, and the people who will provide the care are told before they start.

### 5.2 Behaviour that comes from distress or illness

Many people who become aggressive are frightened, in pain, confused, or unable to tell us what they need — people living with dementia, a learning disability, a brain injury or mental illness. Their care plan sets out how to support them: what upsets them, how to approach them, what calms them, and when to step back. Where behaviour is frequent or severe, we ask for help from health professionals and develop a positive behaviour support plan. See Positive Behaviour Support.

### 5.3 Reducing the risk

Depending on the risk, controls may include changing how or when care is given, sending staff the person knows, two staff instead of one, staff of a particular gender, avoiding known triggers, or asking family members not to be present. In home care, visits may be rearranged or, as a last resort, suspended until a risk can be managed. In a care home, staff carry or can reach a call or alarm system, and colleagues respond to it.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 When someone becomes aggressive

- Stay calm. Speak slowly and quietly, and do not argue, threaten or raise your voice.
- Give the person space. Do not stand over them, block their way out, or touch them unless you need to for their safety.
- Listen, acknowledge how they feel, and offer choices.
- Follow the person's care plan, if they have one.
- If the situation is getting worse, step away. Make sure the person is safe if you can, then leave the room or the home.

### 6.2 If you are in danger

- Leave as soon as you can, taking your phone and keys. In home care, get to your car or another safe place. In a care home, move away and call for help from colleagues.
- Call 999 if you or anyone else is in immediate danger, or has been assaulted.
- Then tell the office or on-call (in home care) or the shift leader (in a care home).

Staff must never use physical force against a person, except the minimum force needed to protect themselves or others from immediate harm, and only as their training and the person's care plan allow. See Restraint and Restrictive Practice.

### 6.3 Reporting

Report every incident the same day — violence, threats, abuse and near misses — through the site's concerns process. Write what happened, what was said, what you did, and any injury. If the incident involved a person we support, also record it in their daily notes.

If you were injured, see a doctor or go to hospital as you need, and tell us. A serious injury, or one that keeps you off work, may need to be reported to the Health and Safety Executive (see Reportable Incidents).

### 6.4 Crime

Assault, threats to kill, sexual assault and hate crime are crimes, whether or not the person who did it lives in a care home or receives care. We support staff who want to report a crime to the police, and the registered manager will help with this. Where the person who acted may lack capacity, the police and the local authority decide how to respond; the incident is still reported.

## 7. After an incident

### 7.1 Support for staff

The manager on duty contacts the member of staff the same day to check they are safe and well, and offers a debrief. Staff may need time off, a change of rota, or someone to talk to. We will not send anyone back to the same person without talking it through with them first. See Staff Wellbeing and Stress.

### 7.2 Reviewing the risk

{{registered_manager}} reviews every incident and decides:

- whether the person's care plan and risk assessment need to change;
- whether it is a safeguarding matter — for example, aggression between residents, or a sign that a person is being harmed or is in crisis;
- whether to involve health professionals, the local authority or the police;
- whether care can safely continue as it is.

### 7.3 Family members and visitors

Where aggression comes from a family member or visitor, the registered manager speaks or writes to them, explaining that the behaviour is not acceptable and what will happen if it continues. In home care, if it continues, we may ask that the person is not present during visits, or end the service following our Ending a Service policy. In a care home, any restriction on a visitor is made only after considering the resident's right to receive visits under Regulation 9A, and is proportionate and reviewed (see Visiting).

## 8. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure risks are assessed and planned for, reviews every incident, supports staff, and decides on action against people who abuse staff.
- **Office, on-call staff and shift leaders** — respond to calls for help, make sure staff are safe, and record incidents.
- **All staff** — follow care plans, take steps to stay safe, report every incident, and support colleagues.

## 9. Training

All staff complete training in managing conflict, de-escalation and personal safety during induction, and refresh it every year. Staff who support people whose behaviour may challenge receive further training, including positive behaviour support and, where it is part of the person's plan, approved physical intervention techniques. Training is recorded on the training matrix.

## 10. Records

Incidents, reviews and actions are kept through the concerns process. Risk assessments and behaviour support plans are kept on the person's care record. All are kept for the periods set in Record Keeping and Retention.
