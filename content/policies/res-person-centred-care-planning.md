## 1. Purpose

In a care home, a resident is supported by many different staff across the week, on days, evenings and nights, many of whom were not there when they moved in. The care plan is how each of them knows the person. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} writes care plans with residents, so that the plan describes the person and the life they want here, tells staff on every shift what to do, and stays up to date as things change.

## 2. Scope

It applies to every resident, including those staying for respite, to everyone who writes or reviews care plans, and to all staff who follow them, including agency staff.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 9: Person-centred care — care is appropriate, meets the person's needs and reflects their preferences, and they are involved in planning it.
- Regulation 11: Need for consent — the plan is agreed with the resident, or made in line with the Mental Capacity Act.
- Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment — risks are assessed and the plan says how they are reduced.
- Regulation 17: Good governance — records about each person are accurate, complete and up to date.
- Care Act 2014 — the wellbeing principle, and the local authority's care and support plan for residents it funds, which ours must fit with.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — residents are supported to make their own decisions about their care.
- Accessible Information Standard — the plan records how the resident communicates and the support they need.

## 4. Our policy

- The plan is written with the resident, in their words where possible. It starts with who they are and how they want to live here, not with tasks.
- It says what the resident can do for themselves, so that staff support independence rather than take it away.
- It covers the whole day and night, so that staff on every shift know the resident's routines and wishes.
- Every risk in the plan has an agreed way of reducing it, and the resident's right to take reasonable risks is respected.
- The resident, and anyone they choose, can see their plan whenever they want.
- The plan is checked every month, reviewed when things change, and fully reviewed with the resident at least every year.

## 5. What a care plan contains

### 5.1 About the person

- How they like to be addressed, and the people who matter to them, including who they would like to visit.
- Their life history, interests, faith, culture and what makes a good day for them, as much as they want to share.
- What they want from living here — company, getting stronger, peace and quiet.
- How they communicate, any sensory loss, their preferred language, and their glasses, hearing aids or dentures.
- Their preferences about who gives personal care, including the gender of staff.

### 5.2 Their decisions

- Whether they have agreed to the plan, or how decisions are made if they cannot.
- Any lasting power of attorney, deputy, advance decision or DNACPR decision, and what it covers.
- Any Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards authorisation, and its conditions.
- Decisions they have made that carry a risk, and what was agreed.

### 5.3 Their day and night

- Mornings: when they like to get up, how they like to wash and dress, and what they do for themselves. Write it so a new member of staff could follow it — for example, "Mr Okafor likes a strip wash at the sink before breakfast and shaves himself with his electric razor. Lay out two shirts so he can choose." Avoid "assist with personal care".
- Meals: where they like to eat, what they like, their diet and drinks, and the help they need.
- The day: how they like to spend their time, activities, going out, and time alone.
- Evenings and nights: when they like to go to bed, their bedtime routine, whether they want the door open or a light on, what checks they have agreed to at night, and what helps if they wake.

### 5.4 Health and safety

- Medicines, with a current list and how they like to take them.
- Moving and handling, with the equipment and method for each transfer.
- Eating and drinking, weight and nutrition screening, skin care, continence, oral health and falls, where they apply.
- Any sensor mat, bed rail or other restriction, why it is used and that it has been agreed.
- Their personal emergency evacuation plan.
- Who to contact — family, GP, district nurse — and what the resident wants them told.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 Writing the plan

Before the resident moves in, the senior writes an initial plan and the key risk assessments from the pre-admission assessment (see Assessment and Starting a Service). Over the first weeks the key worker builds the full plan with the resident and anyone they want involved. The senior reads the plan back to the resident, or shares it in a format they can use, and records whether they agree.

### 6.2 Where the plan is kept

The plan is kept on the resident's record and is available to every member of staff on shift. Treat it as confidential: do not leave it open where other residents or visitors can see it, and do not discuss it in communal areas.

### 6.3 Following the plan

- Read the plan before you first support a resident, and check the notes and handover for changes at the start of every shift.
- Follow the plan, asking the resident at each step. The plan describes what they usually want; it does not replace asking.
- If the resident wants something done differently today, and it is safe, do it their way and record it.
- If something in the plan cannot be done, or does not seem right, do not guess. Ask the shift leader.

### 6.4 Daily records and handover

Record during or at the end of your shift what support you gave, what the resident ate and drank if the plan asks, how they seemed, and anything that has changed or worried you. Write facts, in plain language, as if the resident were reading it — they may. Night staff record checks and how the resident slept. The shift leader passes changes and concerns on at every handover.

### 6.5 Telling us about changes

Tell the shift leader the same day if a resident's needs change: they need more help to stand, they are more confused, they are not eating, or they seem low or withdrawn. The shift leader tells the senior or key worker, who updates the plan, and makes sure the next shift knows.

### 6.6 Monthly check and reviews

Each month, the key worker or a senior checks the plan against the resident's daily records, weight and nutrition screening and dependency assessment, talks with the resident about how things are, and updates anything that has changed. The check is recorded even if nothing changes.

A senior reviews the whole plan with the resident, and their family or representative if the resident wishes:

- at the end of any trial period, or after the first few weeks;
- after a fall, a hospital stay, a new diagnosis, or any significant change;
- when the resident, their family or a member of staff asks;
- and in any case at least every year.

At each review we ask the resident what is working and what is not, and what they would like to change about their life here. If the local authority or the integrated care board funds the placement, we tell them when the resident's needs change and take part in their reviews.

### 6.7 When the resident cannot agree

If the resident cannot agree to part of their plan, that part is made in their best interests, following the Mental Capacity Act, with the people who know them. The plan says which decisions were made this way and why. See Mental Capacity, Best Interests and Advocacy.

## 7. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure every resident has a current plan, checks a sample of plans and daily records for quality, and makes sure checks and reviews happen on time.
- **Seniors** — write initial plans, lead reviews and update plans.
- **Key workers** — get to know the resident, build the full plan with them, and carry out the monthly check.
- **Shift leaders** — make sure staff on their shift know and follow the plans, and pass changes on at handover.
- **Care staff** — read and follow the plan, ask the resident, keep good daily records and report changes.

## 8. Training

All care staff learn how to read and follow a care plan and keep daily records during induction, as part of the Care Certificate. Staff who write and review plans are trained in person-centred planning and risk assessment, and their first plans are checked by the registered manager. Spot checks look at whether staff follow the plan, and are recorded on the person's file.

## 9. Records

Care plans, risk assessments, monthly checks, reviews and daily records are kept on the resident's record. When a plan changes, the earlier version is kept so we can show what was in place at any time. Records are kept for as long as our retention schedule requires (see Record Keeping and Retention).
