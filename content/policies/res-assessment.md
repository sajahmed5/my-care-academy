## 1. Purpose

Moving into a care home is one of the biggest changes in a person's life, and it often happens at a time of illness, loss or crisis. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} decides whether the home is right for a person, how we assess them before they move in, and how we welcome them and help them settle so that the home becomes theirs.

## 2. Scope

It applies to every admission: planned moves, moves from hospital, emergency admissions and short stays for respite. It also applies when a resident returns from a long hospital stay with changed needs.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 9: Person-centred care — care is designed with the person, after an assessment of their needs and preferences.
- Regulation 9A: Visiting and accompanying in care homes — residents can receive visitors, including on the day they arrive, and be accompanied to hospital and outpatient appointments.
- Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment — risks are assessed and reduced, and medicines are managed safely from the day the person arrives.
- Regulation 18: Staffing — we admit a person only when we have enough suitably skilled staff to meet their needs as well as everyone else's.
- Care Quality Commission (Registration) Regulations 2009, Regulation 19: Fees — the person, or whoever pays, receives a written statement of the terms and the fees.
- Consumer Rights Act 2015 and the Competition and Markets Authority's advice to care homes on consumer law — clear information about fees before a decision is made, and fair contract terms.
- Care Act 2014 — the local authority's assessment and care and support plan, and the person's right to choose their accommodation.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — the decision to move into a care home belongs to the person unless it is shown that they cannot make it.

## 4. Our policy

- We admit a person only when we are confident we can meet their needs safely, alongside the needs of the people already living here.
- Nobody moves in without a pre-admission assessment carried out by us. Another organisation's assessment informs ours; it does not replace it.
- The person chooses to come here, or the decision is made lawfully in their best interests. A family's wish alone is not enough.
- Fees, what they cover and how they may change are set out clearly before anyone commits.
- A new resident is welcomed, not processed.

## 5. What to do

### 5.1 Enquiries and visits

We give everyone who enquires our guide to the home, the current fees and what they include, and our standard contract terms, before they decide. We encourage the person to visit, meet residents and staff, see a room and have a meal; where they cannot, we offer a video call and go to them.

### 5.2 Pre-admission assessment

The registered manager, or a senior they have trained, assesses the person where they are living — at home, in hospital or in another service — before any offer is made. We read the local authority's assessment, the hospital discharge summary and the medicines list first. The assessment covers:

- what the person wants from their life here, their routines, interests and what matters to them;
- their health, mobility, personal care, eating and drinking, continence, skin, memory and mood;
- medicines, and who prescribes them;
- communication, sensory loss and language;
- capacity to decide on the move, and any attorney, deputy or advance decision;
- risks: falls, pressure damage, choking, leaving the building unsafely, and any behaviour that could affect other residents;
- equipment the person needs, and whether their room and the building suit them;
- cultural, religious and dietary needs, and preferences about who gives personal care.

### 5.3 Deciding whether to admit

The registered manager decides, considering not only the person's needs but also the current residents, staffing on every shift including nights, and whether we need equipment or training before they arrive. If the person needs nursing care that only a nursing home can provide, we say so honestly. If we cannot meet their needs, we explain why to them and to the referrer.

### 5.4 Capacity and liberty

If the person may not be able to decide for themselves to move here, a capacity assessment and, if needed, a best interests decision are completed and recorded before admission — usually by the local authority or hospital that arranges the move. If their care here will mean they are under continuous supervision and control and not free to leave, the registered manager plans to apply for a Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards authorisation on or before the day they arrive (see Deprivation of Liberty).

### 5.5 The contract

Before admission, the person, or whoever has authority to act for them, receives the contract and the written statement of fees, with time to read it and ask questions. We explain any trial period, the notice either side must give, what happens to fees if the person goes into hospital, and how fees are reviewed. Where the local authority pays, we explain any top-up and who pays it.

### 5.6 Preparing for the day

Before the person arrives, the shift leader for that day makes sure that:

- the room is clean, warm and ready, with any equipment in place and checked;
- an initial care plan and the key risk assessments are written, so staff on every shift know how to keep the person safe from the first hour;
- medicines arrangements are agreed with the hospital, the GP and the pharmacy, so that no dose is missed;
- the person's key worker, or the member of staff who will welcome them, is on shift;
- other residents who will be their neighbours have been told a new person is coming.

### 5.7 Admission day

Plan the arrival for a time that suits the person, not the shift. On the day:

- greet the person by the name they prefer, and introduce yourself and a few residents;
- show them their room, the toilets, the lounge and dining room, and how to call for help;
- offer a drink and a meal, and ask what they like;
- check the medicines that arrive against the discharge or GP list, record them, and resolve any difference with the GP or pharmacist the same day;
- record the belongings they bring, with them or their family, and agree how valuables and money will be kept;
- check their skin, with consent, and record any marks or damage that were already there;
- tell visitors they are welcome, including on the first day;
- spend time with them through the evening; a first night is often the hardest.

### 5.8 Settling in

In the first weeks the key worker gets to know the person and their family and adds to the care plan: life history, routines, likes and dislikes, what helps when they are anxious. Staff note how they are eating, sleeping and joining in. Some people grieve for their old home; give them time and keep familiar things around them.

The registered manager or a senior reviews the placement with the person, and their family if the person wishes, at the end of any trial period or after the first few weeks: is the full care plan complete and agreed, does the person want to stay, and are we meeting their needs? If the move is not working, we say so early and help find somewhere better.

### 5.9 Emergency and short-stay admissions

When a person must be admitted urgently, the pre-admission assessment is still done before they arrive — on the ward or by phone with the professionals who know them — and completed in person the same day. Respite residents are assessed in the same way; if they come back for further stays, we check what has changed each time.

## 6. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — decides on every admission, makes sure pre-admission assessments are done, and makes sure fees and terms are given clearly before anyone commits.
- **Senior or deputy** — carries out assessments when trained to, and writes the initial care plan and risk assessments.
- **Shift leader** — prepares for the arrival, reconciles medicines, and makes sure staff on every shift know the new resident's needs.
- **Key worker** — welcomes the person, gets to know them, and builds the full care plan with them.
- **Care staff** — read the care plan, help the person settle, and report concerns.

## 7. Training

Staff who carry out pre-admission assessments are trained in assessment, risk assessment and the Mental Capacity Act, and are observed doing their first assessments. Staff who check medicines on admission are trained and assessed as competent to do so. Competence is recorded on each person's file.

## 8. Records

The enquiry, the pre-admission assessment, the decision to admit, the contract and fee statement, capacity assessments, the belongings list, the medicines check on admission and the settling-in review are kept on the resident's record for as long as we keep it (see Record Keeping and Retention). Admissions we decline are recorded with the reason.
