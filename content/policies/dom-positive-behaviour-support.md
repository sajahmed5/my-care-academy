## 1. Purpose

Some clients sometimes behave in ways that can hurt them or others, or that limit their lives: shouting, hitting out, refusing care, leaving the house at night, harming themselves. That behaviour almost always has a reason — pain, fear, boredom, a change in routine, not being understood. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} uses positive behaviour support to understand the reasons, improve the person's quality of life, and so reduce the behaviour, rather than simply trying to control it.

## 2. Scope

It applies to every client whose behaviour is described as challenging, or as distressed, including people with a learning disability, autistic people, people living with dementia and people with mental health needs. It applies to everyone who works for us, including care workers who support these clients for long periods in their own homes or in supported living.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 9: Person-centred care, and Regulation 13: Safeguarding service users from abuse and improper treatment — which treats control or restraint that is not necessary or proportionate as abuse.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005, sections 5 and 6 — when acts done to protect a person who lacks capacity are lawful, and the limits on restraint.
- Human Rights Act 1998 — freedom from degrading treatment, the right to liberty and the right to respect for private life.
- Health and Care Act 2022 — registered providers must make sure staff receive training on learning disability and autism appropriate to their role.
- NICE guideline NG11, Challenging behaviour and learning disabilities: prevention and interventions; and NICE guideline NG97, Dementia: assessment, management and support.
- Department of Health, Positive and Proactive Care: reducing the need for restrictive interventions (2014).
- Restraint Reduction Network Training Standards — the standard for any training that includes restrictive practices.
- STOMP — the NHS England programme to stop the over-medication of people with a learning disability, autism or both.

## 4. Our policy

- Behaviour is communication. Our first task is to understand what the person is telling us.
- We improve the person's life — what they do, who they see, how much control they have — because that is what reduces distress over time.
- We plan ahead so that staff know how to prevent distress, how to spot it building, and how to respond calmly.
- We do not punish people, withdraw things they value as a consequence of behaviour, or use threats.
- We use restrictive practices only as a last resort, in line with our Restraint and Restrictive Practice policy.
- We look after the care workers who support people in distress.

## 5. The behaviour support plan

Every client whose behaviour puts them or others at risk, or seriously limits their life, has a behaviour support plan as part of their care plan. It is written with the person, their family and, where possible, a specialist such as the community learning disability team, the community mental health team or a behaviour support practitioner. It is based on an assessment of why the behaviour happens. It sets out:

- the person's life history, what they enjoy, and what a good day looks like;
- what the behaviour looks like, and what it seems to achieve for the person;
- what tends to trigger it — pain, hunger, noise, too many people, unfamiliar care workers, being rushed, a particular task;
- proactive strategies — what we do every day to keep the person well and settled, such as a consistent team, a predictable routine, choice, meaningful activity and good health care;
- early warning signs, and what to do when you see them — offering a break, changing the subject, giving space, offering a preferred activity;
- what to do if the behaviour happens — how to stay safe, how to keep the person and others safe, and when to leave;
- what to do afterwards to help the person recover;
- any restrictive practice that has been agreed, why it is needed, and how it will be reduced.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 Before a visit

Read the behaviour support plan, as well as the care plan, before your first visit. If you are not familiar with the client, the office will arrange for you to shadow a care worker who knows them. Check the notes for recent changes or incidents.

### 6.2 Day to day

Follow the proactive strategies. Keep to the routine the plan describes, explain what you are going to do, offer choices, and do not rush. Notice what the person enjoys and what upsets them, and tell the office so the plan can be improved.

### 6.3 When you see early warning signs

Do what the plan says. In general: stay calm, lower your voice, give the person space, reduce demands, and do not argue. Leave a task for later if it is causing distress and can wait safely. Do not crowd or corner the person.

### 6.4 If the behaviour happens

- Keep yourself and others safe. Move away and give the person space. If you are in danger, leave the home and phone on-call. Phone 999 if anyone is in immediate danger.
- Do not use physical intervention unless it is in the person's plan and you have been trained in it, or as an absolute last resort to prevent serious harm.
- Once the person is calm, follow the plan's recovery steps. Do not discuss the incident with them until they are ready.
- If you leave a client before their care is complete, tell on-call straight away so that the client's safety can be planned for.

### 6.5 Afterwards

Record what happened in the daily notes and through the site's concerns process as an incident: what happened before, during and after, what you did and what helped. These records help us understand the behaviour. Your senior or the on-call manager will talk with you afterwards, and check that you are all right. If you were hurt, or the client was, follow our Accidents, Incidents and Near Misses policy.

### 6.6 Health and medicines

A change in behaviour may be the first sign of pain, infection, constipation, a side effect or another health problem, especially in people who cannot easily tell us. Report changes so a health check can be arranged. Medicine used to manage behaviour must be prescribed, recorded on the medicines record, given only as directed, and reviewed regularly with the prescriber. We support STOMP and ask for reviews where medicine may no longer be needed.

## 7. Reviewing the plan

A senior reviews the behaviour support plan with the client and others involved at the interval the plan sets, after any serious incident, and when incidents become more or less frequent. The registered manager looks at incident records across all clients to spot patterns and make sure restrictive practices are reducing.

## 8. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure behaviour support plans are in place, specialists are involved, staff are trained, and incidents and restrictive practices are reviewed.
- **Seniors** — write and review plans with specialists, introduce new care workers to clients, and debrief staff after incidents.
- **Office and on-call staff** — support care workers during and after incidents, and make sure only trained care workers are rostered to clients who need them.
- **Care workers** — follow the plan, record and report, and tell us what they learn about the person.

## 9. Training

Care workers who support a client with a behaviour support plan complete positive behaviour support training before working with them alone, and are introduced to the client by someone who knows them. All staff complete training on learning disability and autism appropriate to their role, and dementia training. Any training that includes physical intervention is certified against the Restraint Reduction Network Training Standards. Training is refreshed at the intervals on the training matrix.

## 10. Records

Behaviour support plans, functional assessments, incident records, debriefs and reviews are kept on the client's record for as long as our retention schedule requires (see Record Keeping and Retention).
