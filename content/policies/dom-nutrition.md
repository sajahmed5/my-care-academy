## 1. Purpose

Many of our clients rely on care workers for some or all of what they eat and drink, often in short visits, in their own kitchens, with whatever food is in the house. Older people living at home are at real risk of malnutrition and dehydration, which lead to falls, infections, pressure ulcers and hospital admissions. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} helps clients eat and drink well, safely and as they choose.

## 2. Scope

It applies to every client whose care plan includes help with shopping, preparing meals, eating or drinking, or monitoring their intake, and to every care worker who gives that help.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 14: Meeting nutritional and hydration needs — people receive enough to eat and drink, to meet their needs and preferences, with support where they need it.
- Regulation 12: Safe care and treatment — including following the advice of health professionals such as speech and language therapists and dietitians.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005 — people choose what they eat, including foods that carry a risk.
- Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST), published by the British Association for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition — the screening tool we use.
- International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative (IDDSI) framework — the standard descriptions of food textures and drink thicknesses.
- NICE guideline CG32, Nutrition support for adults, and NICE guideline NG21, Home care.
- Food Standards Agency advice on safe food handling at home: cleaning, chilling, cooking and avoiding cross-contamination.
- Resuscitation Council UK guidance on choking, as taught in first aid training.

## 4. Our policy

- Clients choose what they eat and drink, and when, from what is available to them. We respect cultural, religious, ethical and personal preferences.
- Every client's nutrition and hydration needs, risks and preferences are assessed and written in their care plan.
- We prepare food safely in the client's kitchen and do not give them food that may be unsafe.
- We follow professional advice about texture-modified diets, thickened drinks and special diets exactly.
- We notice and report weight loss, poor appetite, signs of dehydration and swallowing difficulties.
- We encourage clients to do as much as they can for themselves, from choosing to cooking.

## 5. Assessment and planning

At assessment, and at each review, a senior records:

- what the client likes and dislikes, their usual routine, and any cultural or religious needs;
- allergies and intolerances, and diets for health conditions such as diabetes or kidney disease;
- who shops for and prepares food, and what the client does for themselves;
- any difficulty with chewing, swallowing, holding cutlery or cups, or sitting upright to eat;
- any texture-modified diet or thickened drinks prescribed, with the IDDSI level and the speech and language therapist's guidance;
- a MUST screen, where the client agrees, with the action it points to.

A MUST screen is repeated when the care plan is reviewed, and sooner if a care worker reports that the client's clothes are looser, they are eating less, or they look unwell. A medium or high score is reported to the GP, who may refer to a dietitian.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 Preparing food in the client's home

- Wash your hands before handling food, and keep the work surfaces, chopping boards and utensils clean.
- Check use-by dates, and the condition of food in the fridge, before you use it. Do not serve food past its use-by date. Tell the client and, with their agreement, throw it away, and record that you have done so.
- Keep raw meat away from ready-to-eat food. Cook food thoroughly until it is steaming hot all the way through, and follow the instructions on ready meals.
- Cool leftovers quickly, cover and label them with the date, and put them in the fridge. Reheat food only once.
- Report a fridge that is not keeping food cold, a cooker that is unsafe, or a kitchen that is no longer clean enough to prepare food in.

### 6.2 Helping a client eat and drink

Offer choices — show the client what is in the cupboard if that helps. Make sure they are sitting upright and comfortable, and have their glasses, teeth and hearing aids. Cut food up or use adapted cutlery if the plan says so. If you help them eat, sit beside them, go at their pace, and tell them what each mouthful is. Do not rush to finish within the visit.

### 6.3 Drinks

Leave drinks within reach at the end of every visit, in a cup or beaker the client can manage, and offer a drink during the visit. Older people often do not feel thirsty. Signs of dehydration include dark urine, a dry mouth, headaches, confusion, dizziness and constipation; report them.

### 6.4 Texture-modified diets and thickened drinks

Prepare food and drinks exactly to the level the speech and language therapist has set. Use the thickener prescribed, measured as directed, and check the texture before giving it. Store thickening powder where the client, and anyone else at risk, cannot accidentally swallow it dry. If a client's swallowing seems to get worse — coughing, choking, a wet or gurgly voice after drinking, chest infections — report it the same day for a review.

A client who can make the decision may choose to eat food that has been advised against. This is recorded as a choice with the risks discussed (see Choice and Positive Risk-Taking).

### 6.5 Choking

If a client is choking, follow your first aid training: encourage them to cough; if they cannot, give back blows, then abdominal thrusts if back blows do not work. Phone 999 if the blockage does not clear, or if the client becomes unresponsive, and start CPR if they are not breathing normally. Anyone who has had abdominal thrusts must be seen by a doctor, even if they seem well. Tell on-call and record it.

### 6.6 Recording and monitoring

Record what the client ate and drank if the care plan asks, using the food and fluid record. Be specific — "half a bowl of soup, two biscuits, 150 ml of tea" rather than "ate well". Report the same day if a client:

- has eaten little or nothing for two visits in a row, or has not had a drink since the last visit;
- has no food in the house, or food that is going off;
- has lost weight, or their clothes or dentures are getting loose;
- has new difficulty chewing or swallowing, or pain in the mouth.

The office contacts the client's family, the GP or the commissioner as needed, and records what was done.

### 6.7 Shopping and money

Shop for food the client has chosen, keep receipts, and follow our Handling Clients' Money policy. If a client cannot afford enough food, tell the office, which can help them get advice on benefits and local support.

### 6.8 Tube feeding

Care workers support feeding through a tube only as a delegated healthcare task, after training and competency assessment by the health professional responsible (see Delegated Healthcare Tasks).

## 7. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — makes sure nutrition and hydration needs are assessed, care workers are trained, and concerns are acted on.
- **Seniors** — complete assessments and MUST screens, write the care plan, and make referrals.
- **Office and on-call staff** — act the same day on reports of poor intake, missing food or swallowing problems.
- **Care workers** — prepare food safely, support clients to eat and drink, record and report.

## 8. Training

All care workers complete training in food hygiene, nutrition and hydration, and dysphagia awareness, including the IDDSI framework, during induction, and refresh it at the interval on the training matrix. Choking is covered in first aid training. Seniors are trained to use the MUST tool before they screen.

## 9. Records

Nutrition assessments, MUST screens, speech and language therapy and dietitian advice, food and fluid records and reports to health professionals are kept on the client's record for as long as our retention schedule requires (see Record Keeping and Retention).
