## 1. Purpose

Every adult has the right to live in safety, free from abuse and neglect. Our care workers are often the only people from outside the family who see a client regularly, in their own home, so they may be the first to notice that something is wrong. This policy sets out how {{organisation}} prevents abuse and neglect, how care workers recognise and report it, and how we work with the local authority so that clients are protected in the way they want.

## 2. Scope

It applies to everyone who works for us, including agency staff, and to every client aged 18 or over. It covers abuse or neglect by anyone: family, friends, neighbours, other professionals, strangers and our own staff. A concern about a child, including a child in a client's household, follows our Safeguarding Children policy.

## 3. The law and guidance

- Care Act 2014, sections 42 to 46 — the local authority must make enquiries, or ask others to, when it suspects that an adult with care and support needs is experiencing, or at risk of, abuse or neglect and cannot protect themselves because of those needs (section 42). Each area's Safeguarding Adults Board sets local procedures, holds safeguarding adults reviews and can require information from us (sections 43 to 45). Section 46 abolished the old power to remove a person from their home.
- Care and support statutory guidance, chapter 14 — the types of abuse and neglect, the six safeguarding principles, and Making Safeguarding Personal.
- Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 13: Safeguarding service users from abuse and improper treatment — systems to prevent abuse and to investigate any allegation immediately.
- Care Quality Commission (Registration) Regulations 2009, Regulation 18 — we notify CQC of abuse, or an allegation of abuse, involving a client.
- Safeguarding Vulnerable Groups Act 2006 — the duty to refer to the Disclosure and Barring Service.
- Mental Capacity Act 2005, section 44, and Criminal Justice and Courts Act 2015, sections 20 and 21 — ill-treatment or wilful neglect by a care worker or a care provider is a crime.

## 4. Our policy

- Safeguarding is everyone's responsibility. Every concern is reported, however small it seems.
- Staff report; they do not investigate. Proving abuse is not your job.
- We follow the six principles: empowerment, prevention, proportionality, protection, partnership and accountability.
- We make safeguarding personal: we ask the client what has happened and what they want to happen, and their wishes shape what we do unless others are at risk or the law requires otherwise.
- Every concern that a client is experiencing, or is at risk of, abuse or neglect is referred to the local authority on the same day.
- We prevent abuse through safer recruitment with enhanced DBS checks, spot checks, supervision every three months, and rotas with enough time for each visit and the travel between them.

## 5. Recognising abuse and neglect

The statutory guidance lists ten types:

- Physical abuse — hitting, pushing, rough handling, misuse of medicines, restraint.
- Domestic abuse — by a partner, ex-partner or relative, including coercive control. It can begin when a partner becomes a carer.
- Sexual abuse — any sexual act or contact the person has not or could not consent to.
- Psychological abuse — threats, humiliation, or cutting someone off from people they want to see.
- Financial or material abuse — theft, fraud, scams, pressure about wills, misuse of a power of attorney or benefits.
- Modern slavery — forced labour, domestic servitude, trafficking, or a home taken over by others for crime.
- Discriminatory abuse — harassment or unequal treatment because of a protected characteristic such as age, disability, race or religion.
- Organisational abuse — poor care caused by how a service is run, such as rushed or missed visits. This includes us.
- Neglect and acts of omission — not providing food, warmth, medicines, help to the toilet or medical care.
- Self-neglect — not caring for one's own health, hygiene or surroundings, including hoarding, to the point of danger.

Signs include unexplained injuries, a client who seems frightened of someone, a relative who will not let you see the client alone, a home suddenly without food or heating, and weight loss or pressure damage with no clear cause.

## 6. What to do

### 6.1 If someone is in immediate danger

Call 999. If the person you think is causing harm is there and you feel unsafe, leave and call from somewhere safe: your safety comes first. Then tell the office or on-call straight away.

### 6.2 If a client tells you something

- Stay calm and listen. Do not ask leading questions or press for detail.
- Tell them they were right to tell you, and that you must pass it on. Never promise to keep it secret.
- Ask what they would like to happen, and pass that on too.
- Do not confront the person who may be responsible.

### 6.3 Report and record

Phone the office on {{office_phone}}, or on-call out of hours, before you leave the visit or as soon as you are safe — not at the end of your shift. Then record it through the site's concerns process as a safeguarding concern the same day: what you saw and heard, in the client's own words where you can, the date and time, and who was there. Use a body map for any injury. Write facts, not opinions. Do not write your concern in any record kept in the home where the person responsible could read it.

If the concern is about the registered manager, tell {{nominated_individual}}. You can always report directly to the local authority or the police yourself.

### 6.4 What the registered manager does

On the same day, {{registered_manager}}, or the manager on-call:

- makes sure the client is safe, and agrees with them what happens now;
- asks for the client's consent to a referral, unless asking would increase the risk;
- refers to the local authority's adult safeguarding team by phone, followed in writing;
- reports to the police where a crime may have been committed;
- notifies CQC (see Reportable Incidents), and tells the commissioner where the contract requires it;
- records each decision and the reasons through the concerns process.

A client with capacity can decline a referral, and we respect that wherever we can. We refer without consent in the cases our Confidentiality policy sets out — for example where others are at risk, a crime has been committed, the client is being coerced, or one of our staff is involved — and tell the client why, unless that would increase the risk.

> Add the adult safeguarding number and the out-of-hours (emergency duty team) number for each local authority you work in.

### 6.5 After a referral

The local authority decides whether to make enquiries under section 42, and may ask us to share records, attend meetings or look into part of the concern. We co-operate, follow the safeguarding plan, and update the care plan. If the response leaves the client unprotected, the registered manager escalates it under the Board's procedures.

## 7. In a client's home

- Family carers — most care well, often under great strain. A carer who is struggling may neglect or harm without meaning to. A referral is not an accusation: the local authority can support the carer too.
- Money — clients are often targeted by scams, doorstep callers and people they know (see Handling Clients' Money).
- Lone visits — you may see what nobody else sees: a room you are never allowed into, a client always asleep when you arrive, bruises in the same place each week. Report it.
- Missed and late visits — a missed visit can leave a client without food, medicines or help to the toilet, and is reviewed as possible neglect (see Missed and Late Visits).
- Self-neglect — keep offering help, record what you see, and report a pattern.
- Restrictions — locking a client in, taking away their walking frame or controlling who visits may be abuse. If care at home may amount to a deprivation of liberty, it needs an order of the Court of Protection, which the local authority or NHS body that arranges the care applies for (see Deprivation of Liberty).

## 8. Concerns about our own staff

An allegation that someone who works for us has harmed a client, or may be a risk to clients, goes to the registered manager straight away, or to {{nominated_individual}} if it is about the registered manager. It is referred to the local authority the same day, under its procedures for concerns about people in positions of trust, and to the police where a crime may have been committed. The worker may be taken off visits to that client, or suspended on full pay, as a precaution (see Disciplinary). Our own investigation never gets in the way of a police or local authority enquiry. We refer to the Disclosure and Barring Service anyone we dismiss, or who leaves, because they harmed or posed a risk of harm to a child or adult, as the law requires.

If you see a colleague mistreat a client, report it. Our Whistleblowing policy protects you.

## 9. Roles and responsibilities

- **Registered manager** — leads on safeguarding, makes referrals and notifications, manages allegations against staff, and keeps local contact numbers up to date.
- **Nominated individual** — receives concerns about the registered manager, and checks that safeguarding is working.
- **Office and on-call staff** — pass every concern to the registered manager at once, or refer directly if they cannot reach them.
- **Care workers** — recognise, report and record concerns the same day, and never investigate or confront.

## 10. Training

All staff complete safeguarding adults training during induction, before they give care, and refresh it every year. The registered manager and anyone who deputises for them complete higher-level training in managing concerns and allegations against staff. Training is recorded on the training matrix.

## 11. Records

Concerns, referrals, decisions and their reasons, and outcomes are kept through the concerns process, linked to the client's record, shared only with those who need to know, and kept for the periods set in Record Keeping and Retention. The registered manager reviews them with all incidents every month to spot patterns.
